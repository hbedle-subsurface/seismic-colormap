Module 03  ·  Colormaps

Perceptual uniformity

A colormap is perceptually uniform when equal steps in the data produce color changes of equal perceived size. Where that fails, some parts of the data range appear to change faster than others, and the fast parts read as boundaries. Those boundaries sit at values fixed by the color table, so they appear on every image ever made with it, in the same place, which is what makes them convincing.

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Equal steps in the data, unequal changes in the color

The blocks below divide the data range into equal steps. Every neighboring pair differs by exactly the same amount, and the numbers under them say so. What the eye reports is a different matter.

Each block is one step. The number of steps and the colormap are set in the panel.

In a uniform map every join looks about as strong as every other, and counting the blocks by eye gives the right answer. In a rainbow some joins are almost invisible while others are hard edges, and counting by eye undercounts in the flat stretches and overcounts where the color turns quickly. The data has equal steps throughout; the display does not.

The curve under the strip in the panel is the same information as a measurement. At each point along the colormap it gives the distance in CIE L*a*b* between one level and the next, normalized so that a perfectly uniform map would sit flat at 1.0. A value of 2 means the color is changing twice as fast there as it does on average; a value of 0.2 means five times slower.

The ratio between the fastest and the slowest part of the map is in the readout. RomaO and vik measure 1.2, Oslo 1.3, grayscale and roma 1.8, viridis 2.4. The traditional rainbow measures 22, and the sixteen-step version is unbounded, because its flat stretches change by nothing at all.

The consequence for interpretation is direct. Two features separated by the same difference in an attribute will look obviously different in one part of the range and identical in another, depending on nothing but where they happen to fall.

2Boundaries that are not in the data

The field below contains no edges. It is a smooth, slowly varying surface: a gentle gradient across the image with one broad rise in it, and nothing discontinuous anywhere. Whatever boundaries appear in it were put there by the colormap.

A smooth field with no discontinuities. The marks above it give the approximate positions at which the local color difference curve peaks, which is where the band edges fall away from the rise in the middle.

In a uniform map the field looks like what it is. In a rainbow it separates into bands with visible edges between them, which is the effect usually called contouring. The bands are the flat stretches of the color table and the edges are the fast stretches, and their positions can be read off the curve in the panel.

The test that settles it is in the panel: move the bottom or the top of the display range. Every boundary in the image slides sideways, because the data value that lands on a given part of the color table has changed. A boundary that belongs to the data cannot behave that way. The channel edge and the fault traces in the seismic map stay exactly where they are while the bands migrate across them.

This is the most useful field test available and it costs nothing: change the display range and watch what moves. Features that hold still are in the data. Features that slide are in the color table. The same check catches a value range so narrow that the attribute is saturating, which is module 06.

The seismic map in the panel is the same story on real features. With a rainbow selected, the envelope develops a rim part of the way down the flank of the channel, at the value where the color table turns quickly. It looks like a boundary in the sand body. Moving the display range moves it to a different part of the flank, and switching to a uniform map removes it and leaves the gradual thinning that is actually there.

3Every map in the library, measured the same way

The chart below runs the same measurement over every colormap in this site. The bar shows the range from the slowest to the fastest part of each map, drawn in the map's own colors, with a uniform map appearing as a short bar sitting on 1.0. Clicking a bar selects that map in the panel.

Click any bar to load that colormap into the panel. The dashed line at 1.0 is where a perfectly uniform map would sit.

The ordering is not a ranking of quality and should not be read as one. A cyclic map and a categorical map are answering different questions, and a diverging map is expected to slow down at its neutral point. What the chart shows is which maps distort the spacing of the data and by how much.

Four patterns are worth picking out.

The tight end is where the designed maps mostly are. RomaO and vik measure 1.2, cool-warm 1.3, Oslo 1.3, roma 1.8, batlow 2.0, viridis 2.4. Grayscale sits among them at 1.8, which is worth noting: it is the oldest display in the library and one of the best spaced, because lightness on its own has nothing to be uneven about.

The rainbows are at the other end. The spectrum maps measure between 6 and 23, and the position of the fast end says where in the range the false edges will fall. The sixteen-step version is the limiting case: its slow end is exactly zero, because sixteen ranges of data values are rendered in a single color each.

A diverging map is slow at its neutral point, by design. Balance measures 4.7, which places it well down the chart, and the slow part is the mid gray at the centre where the map turns around. That is not a defect. A diverging map is meant to compress near zero and spend its color on the departures, and comparing its number against a sequential map's is comparing two different intentions.

The measurement is narrower than it looks. The repeating spectrum measures 3.0, better than balance, while being one of the least usable maps in the library: it runs through the hue circle several times, so two widely separated data values are rendered in the same color. This measurement only asks whether neighboring values are evenly spaced. It does not ask whether distant values are distinguishable, and it does not ask whether any of it can be resolved at all, which is step 4.

4What uniformity does not promise

Two maps are compared below. Both are close to perfectly uniform by the measurement this module has been using. One of them shows the data and the other shows almost nothing.

 

 

The map on the right is the constant-lightness hue ramp built in module 02: lightness fixed, hue sweeping half the color circle, chroma at whatever the display can manage. It passes through more total color than grayscale does, and it spaces that color more evenly. By the measurement in this module it is the better of the two.

It is not a better display, and the reason is that the measurement answers a narrower question than it appears to. Distance in CIE L*a*b* predicts when two patches of color will look different when they are placed side by side and are large enough to fill a decent part of the visual field. It says nothing about how small those patches can become before the difference stops being visible, and that threshold is very different for a difference in hue than for a difference in lightness.

So the two properties are separate, and both are needed.

  • Uniformity says the map does not distort the spacing of whatever it manages to show. A map that fails it invents boundaries and hides real differences in its flat stretches.
  • Resolving power says the map can show fine, low-contrast detail at all. It comes almost entirely from lightness, which is what module 02 measured.

The scientific sequential maps are built to have both: lightness climbing steadily from one end to the other, which delivers the resolution, at an even perceptual rate, which delivers the uniformity. Hue and chroma move alongside to make neighboring values easier to name and to provide a second cue for readers whose color vision is atypical, but nothing is being carried by hue that lightness is not already carrying.

Why it matters

An invented boundary is more dangerous than a hidden feature, because it has the shape of a result. A band edge running across a seismic attribute map follows a contour of the attribute, which means it is smooth, continuous, and frequently parallel to genuine geology. It looks like a fluid contact, a facies boundary or a diagenetic front, and nothing about its appearance distinguishes it from one.

Published interpretations have been drawn along such edges. The check is cheap and takes seconds: change the display range and see what moves.

The hidden half of the same problem is quieter. Where a colormap is flat, real differences are rendered in nearly the same color and are simply absent from the display. Nothing appears in their place, so nothing prompts a second look. This is why comparing an attribute against grayscale is worth doing as a matter of routine rather than only when something looks wrong.

None of this makes a rainbow display worthless. It makes it a display whose boundaries have to be verified before they are interpreted, which is a reasonable thing to ask of any measurement. What it does argue against is choosing a non-uniform map for the initial reconnaissance pass, when the question is what is present rather than how large it is.

Exercises

  1. Count the steps. On step 1, set the colormap to the rainbow and the number of steps to 16, and count the blocks by eye without looking at the numbers. Then do the same in Oslo.
    HintThe rainbow undercounts, usually by three or four, because several neighboring pairs are close to indistinguishable while others are hard edges. Oslo gives the right answer or close to it. Every pair in both is the same distance apart in the data.
  2. Make a boundary move. On step 2, put the rainbow on the smooth field, note where a band edge falls, then raise the bottom of the display range to 20%.
    HintEvery edge slides, because the data value that lands on the fast part of the color table has changed. Nothing in the field has changed at all. A boundary that belongs to the data holds still under the same test, which is what makes it a usable field check.
  3. Find the flat stretch. Select the sixteen-step rainbow and look at the local difference curve, then at the smooth field.
    HintThe curve is zero across each tread and spikes at each riser, and the field separates into sixteen flat bands with hard edges between them. Every one of those edges is at a value fixed by the table. This is the same thing the smooth rainbow does, with the rounding taken off.
  4. Look at a diverging map. Select balance and then red-white-blue, and compare their curves near the middle.
    HintBoth slow down at the neutral point, which is expected: the map is turning around there. Red-white-blue also reaches its greatest lightness there, so zero is the brightest value on the display. Balance turns around at a mid gray, which keeps the emphasis on the large departures in both directions.
  5. Test the limits of the measurement. On step 4, read the two sets of numbers and then look at the two images.
    HintThe hue ramp measures as well or better on every uniformity figure and shows almost nothing. Uniformity is about the spacing of what a map shows, not about whether it shows it, and the second property comes from lightness. A colormap needs both, and neither number alone is enough.
  6. Check a rim. On step 2 with the envelope displayed, find the rim that appears partway down the flank of the channel in a rainbow, then switch to viridis.
    HintIt disappears, and what is left is a gradual thinning toward the channel margin, which is what the model contains. Nothing in the sand body changes at the value where the rim appeared; the rim is the position at which the color table turns from green to yellow.
  • A colormap is perceptually uniform when equal steps in the data produce color changes of equal perceived size. Where it is not, some parts of the range change several times faster than others.
  • Fast stretches become boundaries in the image, at data values fixed by the color table. They appear in the same relative position on every image made with that map.
  • Flat stretches hide real differences, and leave nothing behind to indicate that they have.
  • Changing the display range separates the two kinds of boundary: what belongs to the data holds still, and what belongs to the colormap slides.
  • The measurement predicts differences between large patches seen side by side. It says nothing about how small a feature can be before the difference stops being visible, so a uniform map can still resolve nothing. Uniformity and resolving power are separate requirements.

Method

The local color difference is computed from the colormap table itself. Each level is converted to CIE L*a*b* under D65 and the Euclidean distance is taken between levels eight apart, then divided by the mean of those distances over the whole map. The result is 1.0 everywhere for a perfectly uniform map, and its departures from 1.0 are what the curve plots.

The eight-level stride is there because the tables are stored as eight-bit values. Differencing adjacent levels gives a curve dominated by rounding, with spurious zeros wherever two neighboring entries happen to round to the same byte. Eight levels is wide enough to step over that and narrow enough to keep the features that matter, which are tens of levels wide. The sixteen-step rainbow still returns exact zeros, correctly: its flat stretches are sixteen levels long.

The distance used is CIE76, the plain Euclidean distance in L*a*b*. Later formulae — CIE94, CIEDE2000 — correct known errors in it, particularly for saturated colors and for large differences. They would change the numbers here by some tens of percent and would not change which maps are well spaced and which are not. CIE76 is used because it is the measure the published colormap designs were assessed against, so the numbers here are comparable with theirs.

The color vision figure is the same calculation applied after simulating protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia in turn, reporting the worst of the three. A value near zero marks a stretch of the map that becomes a single flat color for some readers. The simulation uses the matrices of Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009), and module 05 takes it up properly.

The smooth field in step 2 is a linear gradient across the image with one broad Gaussian rise added to it. It is continuous and differentiable everywhere and contains no edges of any kind, so any boundary visible in it has been introduced by the display.
