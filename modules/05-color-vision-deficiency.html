Module 05  ·  Colormaps

Color vision deficiency

Around one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred has some form of color vision deficiency. On a display that carries its information in a red-to-green distinction, that fraction of the room is looking at a different image from everyone else, and nothing on the screen says so. This module simulates the common forms, applies them to the seismic displays built in the earlier modules, and works out which choices survive.

1What is being simulated

Normal color vision uses three cone types, with peak sensitivities in the long, medium and short wavelengths. Color is reconstructed from the differences between their responses. When one type is absent or shifted, some of those differences vanish, and pairs of colors that were distinct become the same.

FormWhat has changedWhat becomes confusableRoughly how common
DeutanMedium-wavelength cones absent or shiftedRed against green at similar lightness; also some greens against graysAbout 6% of men, 0.4% of women
ProtanLong-wavelength cones absent or shiftedThe same red-green pairs, and reds additionally appear darkerAbout 2% of men, 0.03% of women
TritanShort-wavelength cones absent or shiftedBlue against green, and yellow against pinkRare, well under 0.1% and not sex-linked
No colorOnly one functioning cone type, or noneAll hue informationVery rare, about 1 in 30,000

The figures vary by population and by how the condition is defined, and the anomalous forms, in which a cone type is shifted rather than missing, are commoner than the complete ones and vary continuously in degree. The severity slider stands for that: at full severity one cone type is gone entirely, and intermediate settings approximate the milder forms.

The two panels above hold the same display: as it appears on a normal monitor to a viewer with typical color vision, and as it would appear to a viewer with the selected form. Moving the severity slider from zero moves continuously between them.

What survives the simulation is lightness. Protan vision alters it somewhat, darkening reds, and tritan vision alters it very little, but no form of color vision deficiency removes the ability to tell light from dark. That single fact settles most of what follows.

2Which colormaps survive

Each colormap is below as a set of four strips: as displayed, and then under each of the three common forms at full severity. A map that keeps its structure across all four rows is carrying its information in something other than the differences that have been removed.

Top row as displayed, then deutan, protan and tritan at full severity.

The chart below runs the measurement from module 03 after simulation, over every map in the library. The bar is the smallest step that survives, measured against the average step of that same map as displayed. A bar near zero means there is a stretch of the colormap which becomes a single flat color, so a range of data values is rendered indistinguishably. Measuring against the displayed average rather than against the simulated one matters: simulation lowers the average as well as the minimum, and a measure normalized by its own average would report that nothing had happened.

Click any bar to load that colormap into the panel. The simulation used is the one selected in the panel, at full severity.

Three groups come out of it, and the boundaries between them are not where the usual shorthand about colormaps would put them.

Almost nothing is lost. Under deutan simulation the smallest surviving step is 0.85 for Oslo, 0.70 for grayscale, 0.63 for cool-warm and for vik, 0.56 for RomaO and 0.51 for roma. Grayscale is untouched by construction, and the rest carry their data in a lightness ramp with hue added over the top rather than in the hue itself.

Something is lost, and the order survives. Batlow measures 0.38, balance and lajolla 0.30, viridis 0.27. Viridis is worth a note here, because it is often described simply as colorblind-safe. It keeps about 72% of its total color difference under deutan simulation and never goes flat, so the order of values remains readable everywhere. Its smallest surviving step is about a quarter of its average, which means some parts of its range become harder to separate than others. Safe in the sense that matters is not the same as unaffected.

A stretch goes flat. The rainbows measure between 0.02 and 0.10 under deutan, and several of them return exactly zero under protan, which means a range of data values is rendered in one color. The section lost is green through to red, which in a rainbow is the upper part of the range and usually the part holding the anomalies. Red-yellow-green measures 0.11 and is worth singling out, because it is used for risk and probability displays where the entire content of the figure is that the two ends mean opposite things.

3The same data, four ways

The first pair is the rainbow. Under deutan simulation its upper range flattens, and the features that were separated by the green-to-red transition are no longer separated by anything. On the envelope display the channel axis and its flanks merge; on coherence the fault traces lose the contrast that made them followable.

The second pair is Oslo. The two images are not identical — the hue has changed — but every feature visible in the first is visible in the second, and the value order is unchanged. That is the whole of the requirement: nothing has to look the same, and everything has to remain distinguishable.

The test that matters is not whether the simulated image looks pleasant. It is whether a feature that can be located in the top pair can still be located in the bottom pair. Run it on the pockmark field and on the polygonal fault swarm, which are the features nearest the limit of detection and therefore the first to be lost.

The same check applies to a display already chosen for other reasons, and it produces one result that runs against the usual expectation. Take the two arms of a diverging map, a fifth of the way in from each end, and measure how far apart they are. Red-white-blue puts them 160 units apart as displayed, 157 under deutan simulation and 131 under protan: the sign of the attribute stays readable throughout. Red-yellow-green puts them 95 apart as displayed and 18 under deutan, which is close to indistinguishable.

The reason is that red against blue is a difference along the blue-to-yellow direction, which the red-green deficiencies leave largely intact, while red against green is exactly the difference they remove. The traditional seismic polarity convention survives this test; the traditional risk convention does not. Red-white-blue has other problems — its white neutral point is the brightest thing in the image, and its spacing measured 7.9 to 1 in module 03 — but color vision is not among them.

That result is about the blue arm rather than about red-white-blue in particular, and the neighboring conventions in the menu show where the line falls. Blue-white-red and red-black-blue both hold up: their arms stay 91 and 87 units apart under protan simulation. Red-white-green drops from 81 to 6 under deutan, magenta-white-cyan from 100 to 18, and red-green with no neutral at all from 85 to 8. Every one of these has been used as a polarity or risk convention. What separates them is not how modern they look but whether one arm is blue.

Red-green, at the bottom of that list, is instructive for a second reason: it covers 16 points of lightness in total. Even before any simulation it has almost nothing to carry fine detail with, so it fails the module 02 test as well as this one. A map can fail several ways at once, and the measurements in these modules are not substitutes for each other.

4Picks, overlays and legends

A display is rarely just a colormap. Picked horizons, fault sticks, polygon outlines, well symbols, contour lines and legend keys are all drawn on top of it, and every one of them is a color choice made separately from the colormap and usually without any thought about this.

The panel now draws an interpretation over the attribute: the two channel margins, picked where the sand thins to two metres. The hue and lightness of those picks are under slider control, and both panels show the result.

The failure mode is specific. A red pick over a rainbow display is invisible wherever it crosses the red end of the colormap, for everyone. Under deutan simulation it also becomes invisible wherever it crosses the green, which is most of the rest of the image. The pick has not been drawn badly; it has been drawn in a color that the background also contains.

Two things fix it, and they are independent.

Separate the annotation in lightness, not in hue. A pick at L* 15 over a display whose lightness stays above 40 is visible everywhere, in every form of color vision, whatever hue it is. Moving the lightness slider to either extreme while leaving the hue alone demonstrates this directly. This is also why black and white annotation has survived as a convention for so long.

Give the colormap somewhere to be quiet. A colormap that never reaches very dark or very light leaves both ends of the lightness scale free for annotation. Several of the scientific maps stop short of black and white for exactly this reason.

Legends and keys have the same problem in a worse form, because a legend is a small patch of color next to a label and small patches are where color discrimination is weakest. A legend that relies on hue alone to distinguish five categories is asking for a judgement that some readers cannot make, about the smallest objects on the page. Adding a pattern, a letter or a distinct lightness to each category removes the dependency at no cost.

5What the simulation leaves out

Everything so far has treated color vision as having four settings: typical, protan, deutan and tritan. It does not. There is a continuous spread of color vision among people who have no deficiency at all and would pass any clinical test, and there is a second source of variation that has nothing to do with eyes.

The observer

At the light levels of a monitor, color discrimination is done by the cones. Rods are essentially saturated above a few candelas per square metre and contribute almost nothing to color at a screen or in a lit room, although they matter a great deal for what can be seen in a dark one. The variation between people is therefore cone variation, and it is larger than most people expect.

  • The ratio of long- to medium-wavelength cones varies between individuals with normal color vision from roughly one to one up to sixteen to one. Two people with identical clinical results can have retinas with quite different compositions.
  • The long-wavelength photopigment comes in common variants that shift its peak sensitivity by a few nanometres, which moves where in the red-orange range differences are easiest to see.
  • Macular pigment absorbs short wavelengths and its density varies several-fold between people, which changes how blues and violets are weighted.
  • The lens yellows with age. Short-wavelength transmission falls steadily through adult life, so a colormap's blue end is dimmer and less saturated for a reader of seventy than for one of twenty, without either of them having any way to notice.

The consequence for these modules is specific and worth stating plainly. The CIE L*a*b* space, and therefore every uniformity number quoted here, is built on a standard observer: an average of a small group of people measured in the 1920s and 1930s. It predicts what a population does on average. It does not predict what any particular reader sees, and a colormap that measures as perfectly uniform is uniform for an average that no individual is.

The severity slider is the closest thing here to that variation. Anomalous trichromacy is the common case and it is a continuum, not a category, and moving the slider from zero rather than jumping to 100% is a fairer picture of what most affected readers experience.

The screen and the room

The second source of variation acts before any eye is involved, and it is often the larger of the two. The two controls now in the panel set it.

Gamma is the relationship a display assumes between the number in the file and the light it emits. Monitors, projectors and print all differ, and an uncalibrated screen can be some way from what the colormap was designed against. Pushing the gamma above one darkens the middle of the range and compresses the dark end of the map; below one it washes the midtones out.

Light falling on the screen is reflected back off it and adds to everything, which lifts the black point. This is what a projector in a lit lecture room does, and the effect is dramatic: at 15% the bottom quarter of a colormap's lightness range has effectively gone.

Raise the ambient light to 15% and watch the lightness span in the readout. Oslo falls from 100 points of L* to 54, and its smallest step drops to a tenth of what it was. Grayscale falls from 100 to 54 as well and its smallest step goes to zero: the display trusted above all others for edge detection loses its dark end outright in a lit room, because its lowest levels are near-black and near-black is exactly what ambient light destroys. Viridis holds up best of the four, at 0.21, having less of its range invested in the very dark. A colormap chosen on a calibrated monitor is not the colormap the lecture theatre is seeing.

Neither of these is an argument against measuring. The measurements identify displays that fail for everyone, which is worth knowing and is most of the problem. They are an argument against treating a number as a guarantee, and for the one check that costs nothing: look at the figure on the screen it will actually be shown on, and ask the people in the room whether they can see the feature being pointed at.

Why it matters

In a room of twenty-four interpreters, one or two are likely to have a red-green deficiency. They have almost certainly not said so, and many are not aware of it, because the condition is congenital and there is nothing to compare against. What they experience is that some displays are harder work than others and that colleagues occasionally point at features they cannot locate.

The professional consequence is not a matter of comfort. Interpretation is a judgement made from what is visible, and a display that withholds part of its information from some readers produces different interpretations from those readers. Paton (2017) and Nuñez et al. (2018) both make the point that this is a data quality issue rather than an accessibility courtesy.

The fix costs nothing at the point of use. Choosing a colormap whose lightness runs one way is a choice already recommended for the reasons in modules 02 and 03, and it happens to solve this as well. The cases that need specific attention are the ones where hue is doing real work: categorical facies displays, azimuth displays, and anything corendered, which is module 08.

Running a simulator over a figure before it is published or presented takes seconds, and it is the only way to find the problem, because the person making the figure is usually not the person who cannot see it.

Exercises

  1. Lose the channel. Put envelope in the rainbow, set deutan at full severity, and compare the two panels at the channel axis.
    HintThe upper range of the rainbow runs green to red at nearly constant lightness, and that is precisely the distinction deutan vision does not make. The axis and its flanks, separated in the display by that transition, merge into one tone. Everything below the green is unaffected, so the lower half of the range still works and the failure is confined to exactly the part holding the anomaly.
  2. Find the severity where it goes. With the same display, bring the severity up from zero slowly.
    HintThere is no sharp threshold. The separation weakens progressively, which matches the anomalous forms being a continuum rather than a switch. A reader with mild deuteranomaly sees a degraded version of the display rather than a broken one, which is harder to notice and easier to work around badly.
  3. Check a map you expected to fail. Put mean curvature in red-white-blue under deutan, and then in red-yellow-green.
    HintRed-white-blue holds up reasonably: its arms differ in lightness as well as hue, and the white center is the lightest point, so the sign is still readable. Red-yellow-green collapses, because its two arms sit at almost the same lightness and are separated by hue alone. The traditional seismic convention survives better here than the traditional risk convention does.
  4. Read the chart. On step 2, switch between deutan and tritan and watch the ordering change.
    HintMaps built around a blue-to- yellow axis do well under deutan and less well under tritan, and the red-green maps do the reverse. Grayscale is unaffected by all three. A map that is safe under one form is not automatically safe under another, which is why the readout reports the worst of the three.
  5. Hide a pick, then recover it. On step 4, put the display in the rainbow, set the annotation hue to red and the lightness to about 50, and follow the channel margin.
    HintThe pick disappears wherever it crosses the part of the colormap nearest its own color, and under simulation it disappears over a much larger area. Dropping the annotation lightness to 10 or raising it to 90 makes it visible along its whole length regardless of hue, because the separation is now in the dimension that survives.
  6. Try the honest baseline. Set the simulation to no color at all and step through the colormaps.
    HintWhat is left is the lightness profile from module 02, and the maps that still show the data are exactly the ones whose lightness runs one way from end to end. This is a harsher test than any real form of color vision deficiency, and a display that passes it has nothing left to worry about on this score.
  7. Show one of your figures to two colleagues and ask each of them to point at the feature you are describing.
    HintThis is the only test that catches the problem reliably, and it takes less time than the simulation does. A reader who cannot see the feature will usually not say so \u2014 they will assume they are missing something obvious \u2014 so the question has to be asked directly. The people who need to read the figure are the test of whether it works.
  • Around one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred has some form of color vision deficiency. Most have an anomalous form, which is a matter of degree rather than a missing dimension.
  • No form removes the ability to tell light from dark. A colormap whose lightness runs steadily from one end to the other survives all of them.
  • The rainbows fail across their green-to-red section, which is the upper part of their range and usually where the anomalies are. Red-yellow-green fails for the same reason, in a setting where the two ends are supposed to mean opposite things.
  • A map safe under one form is not automatically safe under another, so the worst of the three is the number worth quoting.
  • Annotation is a separate choice from the colormap and fails the same way. Separating picks and overlays from the background in lightness rather than in hue works for every reader.
  • Simulating a figure before it is published takes seconds, and it is the only reliable check, because whoever made the figure is usually not the person who cannot read it.
  • Color vision varies continuously among people with no deficiency at all: cone ratios, pigment variants, macular density and lens yellowing with age all differ. Uniformity numbers describe a standard observer who is an average, not an individual.
  • The screen and the room change the display before any eye is involved. Ambient light on a projector lifts the black point and removes the dark end of a colormap outright.

Method

The simulation uses the model of Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009), which represents each form as a 3 × 3 matrix applied in linear light. The matrices here are the published ones at full severity, and intermediate severities are produced by interpolating toward the identity matrix. The published model provides its own tabulated matrices for each severity step; interpolation is an approximation to those, and it is accurate enough for the comparison being made and not accurate enough to quote a threshold from.

The complete absence of color is simulated by taking the luminance of each color and rendering it as a gray, which is the achromatopsia case. It is far rarer than the other three and is included because it is the strictest possible test of whether a display's information is carried in lightness.

The measurement in step 2 is the one from module 03 applied after simulation, with one change: the local color differences are divided by the average step of the map as displayed rather than by their own average. Normalizing by their own average would hide the effect, since simulation lowers the average along with the minimum, and a map that had lost a third of its color would report the same number as one that had lost none.

The retained-color figure is the summed color difference after simulation over the summed difference before it. It can come out a little above 100% for some maps: the simulation is a linear map in cone space, and although it compresses one direction it can slightly stretch others. Oslo reports 103% under deutan for that reason. Nothing has been gained; a direction that was already carrying no information has been scaled.

A simulation of this kind reproduces which color pairs become confusable. It does not reproduce what a person with the condition actually experiences, which is a lifetime of a different mapping and not a degraded version of someone else's. The purpose here is to find displays that withhold information, and for that the simulation is the right instrument.

The screen model in step 5 applies, in linear light, an ambient term and a power law: the emitted light becomes the ambient fraction plus the remainder scaled by the colormap value raised to the gamma. The ambient term stands for light in the room reflecting off the panel or the screen, which lifts the black point; the power law stands for a display whose response does not match what the file assumes. Both are first-order models rather than device profiles. They reproduce the direction and roughly the size of the effect, and they are not a substitute for looking at the figure on the equipment it will be shown on.

The picks drawn in step 4 are contours of the model's sand thickness at two metres, traced column by column. They stand for any annotation drawn over a display: horizon picks, fault sticks, polygon outlines, contours and well symbols all face the same problem.
