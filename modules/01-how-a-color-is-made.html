Module 01  ·  Colormaps

How a color is made

A colormap turns one number into one color, and a color on a screen is three numbers. This module builds those three numbers by hand, in both directions: by adding light, which is what a monitor does, and by removing it with ink, which is what a press does. The same synthetic attribute map is displayed through whatever color is being mixed, so the effect of every slider is visible on seismic rather than only on a swatch.

The display

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Adding light

A monitor emits red, green and blue light from each pixel and the eye receives the sum. This is additive mixing: more of any channel means more light, all three at full gives white, and all three at zero gives black. The three circles in the panel are the three channels at their current settings, overlapping on a black background.

The strip below them is the ramp running from black to the mixed color, and the map below that is the attribute displayed through that ramp. A ramp built this way is a single-hue ramp: the hue is fixed and the lightness rises steadily, which is the simplest colormap that shows structure.

Three settings are worth producing deliberately.

  • All three channels equal. The mixed color is a neutral gray and the ramp is grayscale. Every feature in the map is carried by brightness alone.
  • One channel at full and the other two at zero. A saturated primary. The ramp still runs from black, so lightness still increases, but it reaches a lower maximum: pure blue is a much darker color than pure yellow, and the map correspondingly has less range to work with.
  • Two channels at full. A secondary color — yellow, cyan or magenta. These are the lightest colors available short of white, which is why they sit at the bright end of so many colormaps.

The lightness readout is L*, on the perceptual scale where 0 is black and 100 is white. Pure blue is close to L* 32; pure yellow is close to L* 97. The two are equally saturated and equally far from gray, and they are nothing like equally bright.

2Removing light with ink

Paper starts white and reflects everything. Each ink absorbs one band of that light: cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. This is subtractive mixing, and it runs the opposite way from the screen. Switching the panel to ink turns the circles over: they now overlap on white, and where all three fall together almost nothing is reflected.

In the idealized model the two descriptions are the same color reached from opposite directions. Cyan coverage is one minus the red channel, magenta is one minus green, yellow is one minus blue. The readouts in the panel hold both at once, and moving either set of sliders moves the other.

Two things make the printed version differ from the screen version.

Black is printed as black. Where all three inks would be laid down together, the neutral part is replaced with black ink instead, which is gray component replacement. The intended color is the same and the amount of ink is less.

There is a limit to how much ink the paper will take. The ink coverage limit slider sets it, and the total for the current color is in the readout. Lowering it below what a color asks for has two stages. First the separation shifts more of the neutral into black, which costs nothing: the color printed is the same. When black can carry no more — which is the case for saturated colors, where the three colored inks have little neutral in common — the colored inks are scaled back instead, and the color changes. The strip in the panel shows the ramp twice: as the screen renders it above, and as the press would lay it down below.

Lower the limit with a saturated color chosen and watch which part of the ramp moves. Blacks and grays hold, because black ink can absorb the whole reduction. Saturated blues, greens and reds give way: they lighten and lose saturation, and the stretch of the ramp they occupy stops separating. That is the usual reason a figure legible on a monitor is not legible in print, and it falls hardest on exactly the saturated colors the rainbow maps are built from.

The model here is the textbook one and is deliberately simple: real inks do not absorb exactly one band each, real paper is not perfectly white, and the set of colors a press can reach — its gamut — is smaller than the model suggests, particularly for saturated blues and greens. The direction of every effect shown here is right; the magnitudes depend on the press.

3A colormap is three functions of one variable

Every colormap in this site is a list of 256 colors, and each of those colors is three numbers. Plotting the red, green and blue channels against position along the map turns any colormap into three curves. The shape of those curves is the whole of its behavior.

The menu in the panel holds both the scientific colormaps and the ones in long-standing use, grouped separately. Comparing the two groups on these curves is more informative than comparing the strips.

A ramp from black to one color is three straight lines starting together at zero and ending at whatever that color is. Nothing can happen in the middle that was not implied by the ends.

Viridis, Oslo and Batlow are three smooth curves that cross at most once. No channel doubles back. That is what allows lightness to increase steadily all the way along the map.

The rainbows are a different shape: each channel rises, holds at full, and falls again, and the three do it at staggered positions. Six corners appear in the curves, and each one is a place where the rate of color change turns abruptly. Those corners fall at fixed positions along the map, which means they appear at the same data values on every image made with it. The sixteen-step rainbow is the extreme case: the curves become a staircase, and every tread is a boundary in the image that the data knows nothing about.

The diverging maps have a plain signature: the channels converge at the center, either to white for the traditional ones or to a mid-gray for balance and vik. A white center is the lightest point in the whole map, so zero is the most conspicuous value on the display even when it is the least interesting.

The strip and the map underneath use the selected colormap, so the corners in the curves can be traced to positions on the strip and then to features in the seismic.

4Building a ramp, and taking the lightness out of it

The panel now shows the ramp's lightness profile below the strip. A ramp from black to a color climbs from L* 0 to the L* of that color; a ramp from white falls the other way. Either is monotonic, which is what a sequential attribute needs.

Switching hold lightness constant on rescales every color in the ramp to the same L*, keeping the hue and saturation as close to the original as the display allows. The profile goes flat. The strip still runs from one color to another and the colors are still visibly different from each other side by side.

The map goes nearly blank.

What survives is a faint tint changing across the image; what disappears is the channel edge, the pockmarks, the fault trace, and the thinning of the sand toward the channel margins. The data has not changed, and the colormap still spans the same two colors. Only the lightness has been removed.

This is why grayscale remains the reference display for edge detection: the visual system resolves fine spatial detail through luminance variation at far higher resolution than it resolves it through hue. Hue is good at telling categories apart. It is poor at showing where an edge is.

Module 02 takes this apart properly, separating hue, lightness and saturation and measuring what each contributes. Module 03 measures whether the lightness climbs at an even rate, which is a different question from whether it climbs at all.

Why it matters

Colormaps are usually chosen from a menu of names. Underneath the names they are all the same kind of object, and the differences between them are visible directly in the three channel curves. A map whose curves are smooth and whose lightness climbs steadily will show gradual change; a map whose curves have corners will put edges at the values where those corners fall.

Knowing the construction also makes it clear what can and cannot be fixed by choosing a different colormap. An attribute whose interesting variation is a few percent of its full range will be hard to see in any colormap, because the problem is the value range rather than the colors. That is the subject of module 06. An attribute whose interesting variation is being hidden by corners in the color table is a colormap problem, and changing the map solves it.

The printing question is smaller but has a habit of arriving late. A figure assembled on a monitor, approved on a monitor, and seen in print for the first time at the proof stage has had no check on whether its dark end survives. Lowering the ink limit in step 2 is that check, and it takes a moment.

Exercises

  1. Compare two primaries. Set the color to pure blue (0, 0, 255) and look at the map, then set it to pure yellow (255, 255, 0) and look again.
    HintBoth ramps start at black and both are single-hue, but the blue ramp ends near L* 32 and the yellow near L* 97. The yellow ramp therefore spans three times the lightness range and shows correspondingly more detail. Saturation is the same in both; what differs is how much brightness the ramp has available between its two ends.
  2. Reach one color from both directions. Mix a mid-green with the light sliders, note the C, M, Y and K readout, then switch to ink and confirm the sliders are already sitting at those values.
    HintThey are the same color described twice. Cyan coverage is one minus red, magenta is one minus green, yellow is one minus blue, and the neutral part common to all three is moved into the black channel. Nothing has been converted or approximated in the idealized model; the two descriptions carry the same information.
  3. Starve the press. Set the color to a saturated blue (0, 0, 255) and lower the ink coverage limit to 150%. Then set a mid-gray and do the same.
    HintThe saturated blue lightens, because cyan and magenta are both near full coverage and have almost no neutral in common for black ink to take over. The gray is unchanged at any limit, because black ink can carry all of it. Saturated colors are the first thing lost in print, which is a problem for any colormap that relies on them.
  4. Find the corners. On step 3, select the rainbow and count the positions along the map where a channel curve changes direction or leaves a plateau, then look for those same positions on the strip and in the map.
    HintThere are six, and they correspond to the cyan, green, yellow and red transitions. Each is a place where the rate of perceived color change jumps, which reads as a boundary. Because the positions are fixed by the color table, the same boundaries appear at the same fractions of the range on every image ever made with that map, which is what makes them look like a property of the data.
  5. Take the lightness out. On step 4, choose any color, then switch hold lightness constant on and off while watching the channel edge in the map.
    HintWith lightness held constant the edge is barely locatable, although the two colors either side of it are plainly different when placed next to each other. The visual system resolves spatial detail through luminance at much finer scale than through hue, so a map that carries its information in hue alone can look colorful and show almost nothing.
  6. Compare the sixteen-step rainbow with the smooth one. On step 3, look at the channel curves of both.
    HintThe staircase makes explicit what the smooth rainbow does more quietly. Each tread is a range of data values rendered in one identical color, and each riser is a step change at a value fixed by the table rather than by the data. The smooth rainbow has the same structure with the treads and risers rounded off, which is why contouring appears in rainbow displays of smoothly varying attributes.

Key points

  • A screen adds light and a press removes it. In the idealized model the two are the same color described from opposite ends, and the conversion between them is subtraction.
  • A colormap is three functions of one variable. Its behavior is visible in the shape of those three curves, and corners in them become boundaries in the image at fixed data values.
  • The primaries are not equally bright. Pure blue is near L* 32 and pure yellow near L* 97, so single-hue ramps built on different hues have very different amounts of lightness to work with.
  • Black ink replaces the neutral part of a three-ink mixture, and a total ink limit compresses the dark end of a colormap. A figure checked only on a monitor has not been checked for print.
  • Remove the lightness variation from a colormap and the image goes nearly blank, however colorful it remains. Spatial detail is carried by luminance.

Method

The mixing figure draws the three channels as overlapping disks. In light mode they are drawn on black with the composite operation set to add, so overlapping regions sum; in ink mode they are drawn on white with the composite set to multiply, so overlapping regions absorb in turn. The swatch beside them is the resulting color, and it is the same in both modes.

The ramp is built in linear light rather than in the stored sRGB values, so that a point halfway along it carries half the light rather than half the encoded number. Lightness is CIE L* under D65. Holding lightness constant scales each entry in linear light, by bisection, until its L* matches the target, and clamps where the result leaves the displayable range — which is why a strongly saturated color cannot be held at a lightness far from its own.

The print simulation treats absorption in each band as the colored ink plus the black, so a color returns exactly until something is clamped. Black generation moves 60% of the neutral common to the three colored inks into black to begin with, and moves more if the coverage limit demands it, since each unit of black saves two units of colored ink. Only when black has taken all the neutral there is are the colored inks scaled back, and that is the point at which the color changes.

Two consequences of the simplification are worth stating. Coverage in this model tops out near 200%, whereas presses usually quote a limit between about 240% and 340%, because real separations carry more ink than neutrality alone requires, for density and for trapping. And real inks are not perfectly selective, real paper is not perfectly white, and the set of colors a press can reach — its gamut — is smaller than this model suggests, particularly for saturated blues and greens. The numbers on the slider are therefore on the idealized scale rather than a press's own. Which colors give way first, and in what order, is not affected.

Ramps built in this module are installed as ordinary colormap tables through Colormaps.define, so the map view, the color bar and the strip all handle them with the same code that handles the published maps.

On the colormaps in the menu

The menu separates maps designed against published perceptual criteria from maps that have been in common use for decades. The second group is there to be examined, not recommended. Several of them are or have been the default in widely used interpretation software, and a large part of the published seismic attribute literature is displayed in them; none of the reconstructions here is copied from any particular implementation.

Two fields in each colormap's record are claims about provenance rather than measurements: whether the map was designed for perceptual uniformity, and whether it was designed for color vision accessibility. The rest — the L* range it covers, whether its lightness runs one way from end to end, the spread of its local color difference, and the worst of that spread under simulated color vision deficiency — are computed from the table itself and can be checked in module 03.
