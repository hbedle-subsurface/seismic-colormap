Module 02  ·  Colormaps

Hue, lightness and saturation

Module 01 built colors from three channels of light. Those channels are what a display is made of, not what the eye reports. Perception separates a color into lightness, chroma and hue instead, and those three are not interchangeable: one of them carries fine spatial detail, one carries order, and one carries category. A colormap that puts its information in the wrong one looks colorful and shows very little.

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Three dimensions instead of three channels

The same colors described in module 01 as amounts of red, green and blue light can be described instead as a lightness, a chroma and a hue angle. Nothing is lost in the translation and the colors are identical. What changes is that each of the three now corresponds to something the visual system actually separates.

The three sliders set one color. The panel then builds a ramp from it by varying whichever dimension is selected across the full data range and holding the other two fixed, and displays the attribute through that ramp. Switching between the three with the sliders left alone is the experiment this module is built around.

Two things show up immediately in the readouts.

Chroma is not freely available. A display can only produce so much chroma at a given lightness and hue, and the limit moves a great deal: near L* 40 a magenta can reach a chroma above 100, while a cyan at the same lightness stops around 30. Asking for more returns the most the display can manage at that lightness rather than shifting the lightness to accommodate it, which is why the chroma readout sometimes sits below the slider.

The three are not symmetric. The lightness ramp spans a fixed range from dark to light whatever the hue. The chroma ramp spans from gray to whatever chroma is available, which may be a great deal or very little. The hue ramp spans half the color circle and, by construction, no lightness at all.

2Which dimension carries fine detail

The figure below is a test pattern rather than seismic. It holds a single sine wave whose spatial frequency increases from left to right and whose amplitude increases from top to bottom. Every column contains the same wave at a different scale, and every row the same scale at a different amplitude. Rendered through a ramp, the boundary where the pattern stops being visible traces out what that ramp can and cannot resolve.

Frequency increases to the right; amplitude increases downward. The ramp is the one selected in the panel, so switching between lightness, chroma and hue changes this figure and the seismic map together.

With lightness varying, the visible region reaches far to the right and high up the figure: fine detail survives at small amplitudes. The upper boundary is a curve rather than a straight line, because the eye is most sensitive at intermediate scales and less so at both extremes.

With chroma varying, the visible region shrinks. The pattern holds at coarse scales and disappears sooner as the bars narrow.

With hue varying at fixed lightness, most of the figure is blank. The coarse, high-amplitude corner remains, and everything fine or faint is gone. The wave is still there in the numbers, and the two colors at the extremes of the ramp are plainly different when placed side by side. They stop being distinguishable as soon as the region carrying them becomes small.

The seismic map in the panel behaves the same way, because it is the same question asked of real features. The channel edge, the fault traces, the pockmarks and the thinning of the sand toward the margins are all fine, low-amplitude detail. They are legible in the lightness ramp, weak in the chroma ramp and effectively absent in the hue ramp.

The readout in the panel gives the total color difference along the ramp: the summed distance in CIE L*a*b* from one end to the other, which is how much color the ramp passes through in total. At the default settings the hue ramp measures around 190 and the lightness ramp around 160. The hue ramp passes through more color, and it is the one that shows nothing.

That is worth stopping on, because it is the limit of what a color-difference measurement can tell you. CIE L*a*b* was built to predict when two patches of color, seen side by side and large enough to fill a good part of the visual field, will look different. It says nothing about how small those patches can be before the difference stops being visible, and that threshold is very different for hue than for lightness. A colormap can be long in color-difference terms, evenly spaced along its whole length, and still unable to resolve a fault trace.

The visual system samples luminance at much finer spatial scale than it samples color. The cells that report brightness are more numerous and their receptive fields are smaller, and both the eye's optics and the compression applied to almost every image format discard high-frequency color information while keeping high-frequency brightness. A display whose information is carried in hue is being fed through the low-resolution channel.

3Which dimension carries order

Detail is one requirement. A sequential attribute has another: the reader has to be able to tell which of two colors stands for the larger value, without consulting the color bar every time. Below are six swatches drawn from the current ramp and shuffled. The question is whether they can be put back in order by eye.

With lightness varying, the order is immediate and no two people disagree about it. Darker is one end and lighter is the other, and that mapping is not learned.

With chroma varying, the order is available but slower: gray at one end and the most vivid version of the hue at the other. Intermediate swatches are harder to separate, and the confident part of the judgement is concentrated near the gray end.

With hue varying, there is no order to find. Whether green comes before orange is a convention, not a perception, and different readers will answer differently and be equally correct. Any ordering a reader does produce has been learned from a color bar or from familiarity with a particular color scheme, which means it transfers to no other scheme and holds only as long as the color bar is in view.

This is what makes hue the right dimension for the one job the other two do badly. A facies classification, a set of seismic facies from an unsupervised classification, a fault-block label: these are categories, not magnitudes, and an ordering imposed on them would be a false claim about the data. Distinct hues at similar lightness say nothing about which class is larger, and that is exactly what is wanted.

4What a published colormap does with all three

Every colormap in the menu can be decomposed the same way: lightness, chroma and hue angle along its length. Module 01 plotted the red, green and blue channels, which describe the display. These three describe what the reader gets.

Lightness in red, chroma in slate. Both on the same axis, since both are measured in the same units.

Hue angle, which wraps at 360°. A jump from one edge of this plot to the other is continuous in color and is not a discontinuity.

The scientific sequential maps put a straight line through the lightness plot from one end to the other, and move hue steadily and chroma modestly alongside it. The lightness does the work of showing the values; the hue makes neighboring values easier to name and tell apart, and adds a second cue for readers whose color vision is atypical. Nothing is being carried by hue that lightness is not already carrying.

The rainbows reverse the arrangement. Hue sweeps almost the full circle, chroma stays high throughout, and lightness rises and falls several times. The plots make the consequence visible: a lightness profile with a peak in the middle means two different data values are rendered at the same brightness, so they look equally far from the ends even when one is near a maximum and the other near a minimum. The eye reads the peak as a ridge in the data.

The diverging maps show a symmetric V or inverted V in lightness, which is correct for an attribute with a meaningful zero: distance from the centre is what should read as magnitude, and lightness is what reports it. The traditional red-white-blue maps take the lightness all the way to 100 at the centre, which makes zero the brightest thing in the image. The scientific ones stop at a mid gray, which leaves the centre quiet.

Whether the lightness merely rises, or rises at an even rate, is a further question. That is perceptual uniformity, and module 03 measures it.

One caution carries forward from step 2. Perceptual uniformity is measured from color differences between large patches, so a colormap can score well on it and still resolve nothing: the constant lightness hue ramp built in this module is almost perfectly uniform by that measure, and the cyclic RomaO is genuinely uniform by design and also runs at nearly constant lightness. Uniformity says that the map does not distort the data it manages to show. Whether it shows the data at all is the question this module asks, and the two are independent.

Why it matters

The most common way a seismic display fails is not that it is ugly or old-fashioned. It is that the quantity of interest has been assigned to hue, and hue cannot resolve it. The display is then genuinely colorful, genuinely varied, and quietly missing the small features it was made to show.

That failure is invisible from inside. A rainbow display of coherence looks detailed, because hue changes rapidly and the image is full of variation. Whether the variation corresponds to the variation in the data is a separate question, and comparing the same data in grayscale is the cheapest way to answer it. If features appear in grayscale that were not visible in the rainbow, they were being carried by a channel that could not deliver them.

The same reasoning settles where hue does belong. Classification output, facies volumes and any labelled result should be displayed in distinct hues at similar lightness, because the classes have no order and any lightness ramp applied to them would assert one. Module 07 takes that up.

It also explains why so many well-designed colormaps look, at first, less impressive than the rainbow they replace. They spend most of their variation on lightness, which the eye reads as structure rather than as color. The image carries more information and looks calmer, and those two things are related.

Exercises

  1. Run the substitution. Set the attribute to coherence and step through the three ramps with the sliders untouched, watching the polygonal fault swarm.
    HintIn the lightness ramp the small offsets are visible as faint lineaments. In the chroma ramp they are marginal. In the hue ramp they are gone, along with most of the channel edge, even though the image is as colorful as before. The data and the value range have not changed in any of the three.
  2. Find the chroma ceiling. Set the hue to about 250° and the lightness to 30, then push chroma to its maximum and read the number returned. Do the same at hue 330°.
    HintThe blue-green direction runs out of chroma long before the magenta direction does, at every lightness. This is a property of the display rather than of perception, and it is why single-hue ramps built on some hues look washed out while others look vivid. The colormap cannot use chroma that the display cannot produce.
  3. Read the test pattern. On step 2, compare where the pattern disappears in the lightness ramp and in the hue ramp, and note roughly how far right and how far up each one reaches.
    HintThe lightness ramp resolves finer bars and weaker amplitudes by a wide margin. The upper edge of its visible region curves over, because sensitivity falls off at both very coarse and very fine scales rather than increasing without limit. The hue ramp keeps only the coarse, strong corner: it needs both a large region and a large difference before it reports anything.
  4. Try to order the hues. On step 3, put the ramp on hue, shuffle, and write down an order before revealing it. Then shuffle again and repeat.
    HintAny order produced comes from the color bar or from habit rather than from the colors themselves, and it is common to give two different answers on two shuffles of the same six swatches. The lightness version does not behave this way: the answer is the same every time and takes no effort.
  5. Decompose a rainbow. On step 4, look at the lightness plot for the rainbow and find the value where it peaks, then find that position on the color bar in the panel.
    HintThe peak is in the yellow, a little past the middle of the range. Data at that value is rendered brighter than data at either end, so a smoothly increasing attribute appears to have a bright ridge partway up its range. Nothing in the data corresponds to it, and it appears in the same relative position on every image made with that colormap.
  6. Compare two diverging maps. On step 4, look at the lightness plots for red-white-blue and for balance.
    HintBoth are symmetric, which is right for an attribute with a meaningful zero. Red-white-blue reaches L* 100 at the centre, so zero is the brightest value on the display and draws the eye to wherever the attribute happens to cross it. Balance stops in the mid range, which leaves the centre quiet and keeps the visual emphasis on the large departures in both directions.
  • Lightness, chroma and hue are the dimensions the visual system separates. A colormap can be described in them just as completely as in red, green and blue, and the description says more.
  • Fine spatial detail is carried by lightness. Chroma carries less of it and hue carries very little, which is measurable on a test pattern and visible on seismic.
  • Order is carried by lightness without being learned. Chroma carries a weaker version of it. Hue carries none, and any ordering a reader produces has come from the color bar.
  • Hue is the right dimension for categories, precisely because it asserts no order.
  • Chroma is limited by the display, and the limit depends strongly on lightness and hue. A ramp built on a hue where little chroma is available cannot be made vivid by asking for more.
  • Comparing a display against the same data in grayscale is the quickest test of whether its information is reaching the reader.
  • A colormap can pass through a great deal of color, spaced perfectly evenly, and still resolve nothing. Color-difference measurements describe large patches seen side by side; they say nothing about how small a feature can be before the difference stops being visible.

Method

Colors are built in CIE L*C*h under D65, the cylindrical form of CIE L*a*b*: lightness, chroma as the distance from the neutral axis, and hue as the angle around it. This is used in preference to the HSL and HSV models familiar from graphics software, whose lightness and saturation are computed from the display's channels rather than from perception, and whose lightness therefore varies by a factor of three across a single hue circle at a fixed setting.

A color asked for outside the range the display can produce is brought back by reducing its chroma and leaving lightness and hue as they are, by bisection on the gamut boundary. The result is that the lightness requested is always the lightness delivered, which is what makes the hue ramp a fair test: it is genuinely at constant lightness along its whole length, and its chroma varies only because the available chroma varies with hue.

The test pattern is a chirped sinusoid: spatial frequency increases geometrically from left to right, with the phase accumulated column by column, and amplitude increases from top to bottom. The arrangement is that of Campbell and Robson (1968), where the boundary of the visible region traces the observer's contrast sensitivity. Here the pattern is rendered through the ramp under construction, so the boundary depends on the ramp as well as on the observer, which is the point.

What the pattern shows on a screen depends on the screen, on the viewing distance and on the observer, so the absolute position of the boundary is not meaningful. The comparison between the three ramps, made without moving, is.
