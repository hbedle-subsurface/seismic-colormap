Module 09  ·  Colormaps

RGB and CMY blending

Module 08 gave a second attribute one of the display's dimensions. A blend gives all three away at once: three attributes drive the three channels directly and no colormap is involved. It is the densest display in common use and the hardest to read, and the most familiar example of it is the spectral decomposition blend.

The three inputs

How they are combined

Additive is what a screen does: a high value adds light to its channel. Subtractive is what a press does: a high value lays down ink and removes light.

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Three attributes, three channels

There is no colormap here. Each attribute is normalized to its own range, multiplied by a gain, and written straight into one of the three channels of the display. The three images below are those channels on their own, before they are combined.

Each is a grayscale display of one attribute, which is the most legible single-attribute display there is. The blend in the panel above is the three of them summed as light. Everything the blend can show is present in these three images; what the blend adds is the ability to see all three at once, and what it removes is the ability to read any of them.

The gain controls are where blends are usually won or lost. Each channel is normalized to its own attribute's range, so a channel whose attribute is mostly low contributes little and the blend takes on the color of whichever channel happens to be busiest. Raising that channel's gain balances it; the readout says how much of the survey each channel is saturating, which is the signal that a gain has gone too far.

A blend in which one channel saturates over most of the survey is not showing three attributes. It is showing two, tinted. The saturation readout is the quickest check, and anything above about 20% on a channel is worth looking at.

2Reading a blend

A blend has no color bar, because there is no single quantity for one to describe. What it has instead is eight corners, and every color in the image is somewhere between them. The strip under the panel names them and gives the share of the survey sitting near each.

ColorMeans
BlackAll three low
WhiteAll three high
Red, green or blueThat one channel high, the other two low
CyanGreen and blue high, red low
MagentaRed and blue high, green low
YellowRed and green high, blue low

Two consequences follow, and both are worth stating before anyone tries to interpret a blend.

Black and white are not extremes of one thing. On a sequential display, dark and light are opposite ends of a single scale. In a blend they are opposite corners of a cube, and the six other corners are equally far from both. A reader who carries the sequential habit into a blend will read brightness as magnitude, and brightness in a blend is the sum of three magnitudes.

The secondary colors carry the information. A feature that appears red is telling you something about one attribute. A feature that appears cyan is telling you something about the other two, and about the absence of the first. In practice most of what is interesting in a blend is in the cyans, magentas and yellows, because those are where two attributes agree and the third does not.

This is also why the ordering of attributes to channels changes a blend so much. The rotate button in the panel cycles the three inputs without changing any of them. The information content is identical and the image looks completely different, because the human reading of red and cyan is not symmetric: red advances and cyan recedes, and whichever attribute lands on red will look like the subject of the figure.

3The spectral decomposition blend

The blend most seismic interpreters meet first is a spectral decomposition, which puts three frequency bands into the three channels. The three inputs in the panel are now the strength of the reflection from this model at a low, a middle and a high peak frequency, which is a simplified spectral decomposition of the same synthetic.

What it shows is bed thickness, by way of tuning. A bed reflects most strongly at the frequency whose quarter wavelength matches its thickness, which is tuning, so a thick bed is brightest in the low band and a thin one in the high band. Red for low, green for middle and blue for high is the usual convention, and it gives the result its familiar reading.

AppearsMeansIn this model
RedTuned at low frequencyThe thickest part of the channel, along its axis
Green through cyanTuned at middle to high frequencyThe flanks, where the sand has thinned
BlueTuned at high frequencyThe channel margins, close to pinch-out
WhiteStrong in all three bandsAn isolated strong reflection, where the whole bandwidth responds
BlackWeak in all threeAway from the sand, and in the pockmarks

Moving the thickness slider is not available here, because the three bands are computed once. What can be done instead is to look at where the color changes along the channel and compare it against the sand thickness attribute, which is in the input menus and is a property of the model rather than a measurement. The color sequence from the axis outward follows the thinning, and it does so without anyone having picked a horizon or measured an isochron.

This is the strongest argument for blends, and it is worth being precise about why. Three bands of the same attribute are not three independent things. The correlation readout says so bluntly: the low and middle bands here correlate at 0.96, because both of them respond to whether there is a reflection at all. A blend of three attributes that correlated that strongly would normally be a two-channel display pretending to be three.

What rescues it is that the reading of a spectral blend is not about any band's absolute value. It is about which band is strongest relative to the other two, and that comparison is meaningful however strongly the three rise and fall together. The blend is not showing three attributes; it is showing one attribute as a function of frequency, and color is a reasonable way to encode where the peak of that function lies. The reading is qualitative — thicker here than there — and that is the reading a spectral blend supports.

It also explains why the gains matter more here than anywhere else. Balancing the three bands so that none dominates is what lets the relative comparison come through, and it is why spectral blends are routinely normalized band by band before being displayed.

The bands here are made by convolving the same reflectivity with wavelets of three different peak frequencies, which is the simplest thing that behaves correctly. Real spectral decomposition uses a time-frequency transform and produces bands from the recorded data rather than from a model, but the tuning behavior that makes the display readable is the same.

4Where blends mislead

A blend is the densest display in common use, and four things go wrong with it often enough to be worth checking every time.

Correlated inputs. Three attributes that measure nearly the same thing produce a blend along the gray diagonal of the color cube: bright where all three are high, dark where all are low, and almost no color anywhere. It looks like a competent grayscale display and it has cost three attributes to make. The correlation readout in the panel gives the largest pairwise correlation between the three inputs. Envelope and sweetness in this model correlate at 1.00, and envelope and sand thickness at 0.91: any blend built from two of those is a two-channel display.

The figure is a flag rather than a verdict, and the spectral bands in step 3 are the case that shows why. They correlate at 0.96 between the low and middle bands, which by the reading above would condemn them, and the blend works anyway. The reason is that the correlation is measuring whether the three channels rise and fall together in absolute terms, and three frequency bands necessarily do: all three respond to whether there is a reflection at all. What the blend is showing is which band is strongest relative to the other two, and that survives a high correlation intact.

So the readout is asking a specific question: are two of these channels carrying the same information? A high number means yes unless the three inputs are related in a way that makes their differences meaningful, which is exactly the situation a band decomposition is in and is not the situation envelope and sweetness are in.

Gains chosen by eye. Every gain setting produces a plausible image, and there is no setting that is objectively correct, so the gains are usually adjusted until the picture looks right. That is a decision about which features appear and it is almost never recorded. Two people blending the same three volumes will not produce the same figure.

Color vision deficiency. A blend is the worst case, because all of its information is in hue by construction. The simulation buttons in the panel show what happens: the red and green channels collapse into each other and the blend becomes a two-channel display, and unlike a colormap there is no alternative version that fixes it. A blend cannot be made accessible; it can only be accompanied by the single-channel displays.

Printing. The subtractive model in the panel is the honest version of what happens when a blend is printed. Switch to it and the image inverts in character: the corners swap, black becomes white, and the saturated colors that carried the message on screen are the ones a press reproduces least well, as module 01 showed. A blend designed on a monitor and printed without checking is likely to lose exactly its most informative areas.

The check that covers most of this is the one from module 08, applied three times: look at each channel on its own first. The three grayscale images in step 1 are more informative per attribute than the blend is, and anything in the blend that is not in one of them is an artefact of the combination.

Why it matters

Blends are persuasive. They are dense, colorful and visibly the product of effort, and they appear in presentations for those reasons as much as for what they show. That makes it worth being clear about what a blend is good for and what it is not.

It is good for one thing in particular: showing that three quantities vary together in a structured way across a survey, when the structure is what matters and the values are not. The spectral decomposition case is the clearest example, because the three inputs are the same attribute at three frequencies and the color sequence corresponds to something physical.

It is poor at anything quantitative, and it is poor at anything that has to survive being printed, projected, or read by a colleague with a red-green deficiency. It is also poor at being reproduced: a blend is defined by three attributes, three ranges, three gains and an assignment of attributes to channels, and that is eight pieces of information which are almost never written down.

In practice a blend belongs in a figure next to the single-channel displays rather than in place of them. Use it to find where the interesting parts of the survey are, and read the values off the separate displays.

Exercises

  1. Make a gray blend. Set all three channels to attributes that measure similar things — envelope, sweetness and sand thickness — and read the correlation figure.
    HintThe blend is nearly gray everywhere, because all three channels rise and fall together and the result sits along the diagonal of the color cube. Envelope and sweetness correlate at 1.00 in this model and envelope and thickness at 0.91, so the readout reaches its maximum. Three attributes have been spent to produce something a single grayscale display would have shown better.
  2. Find the exception. Now set the three frequency bands and read the correlation figure again, then look at the image.
    HintThe correlation is 0.96 and the blend is full of color, which contradicts the previous exercise. Three frequency bands all respond to whether there is a reflection, so they rise and fall together; what the display shows is which of them is strongest relative to the others, and that comparison survives the correlation. The readout is a flag to investigate rather than a verdict.
  3. Saturate a channel. Take one gain to 3 and watch the saturation readout and the image.
    HintThat channel reaches its maximum over a large part of the survey, and everywhere it does the channel has stopped carrying information: the variation is still in the data and no longer in the picture. The blend then shows two attributes over a tinted background.
  4. Rotate the inputs. On any blend, press the rotate button three times and note that the image returns to where it started.
    HintNothing about the data changed at any point, and each of the three versions suggests a different attribute is the subject of the figure. Whichever attribute is on red reads as foreground. This is worth knowing before choosing which attribute goes where, and worth stating in the caption.
  5. Read the channel sequence. On step 3, follow the color from the channel axis out to the margin, then compare it with the sand thickness input.
    HintRed along the axis where the sand is thickest, through green, to blue at the margins where it is thinnest. The color sequence is tracking bed thickness through tuning, without a horizon having been picked or an isochron computed, which is what makes spectral blends useful for channel systems.
  6. Break it under simulation. On step 3, switch the simulation to deutan.
    HintThe red and green channels merge, so the low and middle bands become one channel and the blend loses the distinction between thick and moderately thick. There is no version of the blend that fixes this, which is the difference between a blend and a colormap: a colormap can be swapped for a better one.
  7. Print it. Switch the model to subtractive and look at where the survey is darkest and lightest.
    HintThe corners have swapped: where all three attributes are high the inks combine and the paper goes dark, and where all three are low the paper is left white. Every reading rule from step 2 is reversed, and the saturated colors that carried the message on screen are the ones a press reproduces worst.
  • A blend uses no colormap. Three attributes are normalized, gained and written into the three channels directly.
  • There are eight corners rather than two ends. Black and white are opposite corners of a cube, not opposite ends of a scale, and most of the useful information is in the cyans, magentas and yellows.
  • Which attribute goes on which channel changes the apparent subject of the figure without changing any of the data.
  • Correlated inputs usually produce a gray blend that looks competent and carries one attribute's worth of information. The exception is a set of bands of one attribute, where the reading is which band is strongest relative to the others and a high correlation is expected.
  • The gains decide which features appear and are almost never recorded. A blend needs eight pieces of information to reproduce.
  • A blend puts all of its content in hue, so it fails under red-green color vision deficiency and there is no better blend to switch to.
  • The spectral decomposition case works because its three inputs are one attribute at three frequencies, and the color sequence follows bed thickness through tuning.

Method

Each input is normalized to its own display range across the survey, multiplied by its gain, and clamped to the interval from zero to one. In the additive model that value becomes the channel directly. In the subtractive model it becomes an ink coverage and the channel is one minus it, which is the idealized relationship from module 01.

The channels are written in the display's own encoding rather than in linear light. That is what every implementation of this does, and it is worth knowing that it is a convention rather than a physical statement: a blend is not a measurement of light, so there is no correct answer, and doing it in linear light would produce a different and equally defensible image.

The three bands in step 3 are the envelope of the same reflectivity convolved with Ricker wavelets at peak frequencies of 18, 30 and 48 Hz, all displayed over a common range so that their relative strengths are comparable. This is not a time-frequency transform; it is the simplest construction that reproduces the tuning behavior a spectral decomposition blend depends on, computed from the model rather than from a recorded trace.

The correlation figure is the largest Pearson correlation among the three pairs of inputs, computed over the whole survey. The saturation figure is the largest share of cells reaching the top of any one channel. The corner shares in the legend classify each cell by whether each channel is above or below its midpoint, which is a coarse summary and is meant as one.

The color vision simulation is applied to the finished blend, as in module 08, because it is a property of the reader rather than of any channel.
