Module 04  ·  Colormaps

Sequential, diverging, cyclic

Which class of colormap an attribute needs is decided by the attribute, not by taste. Two questions settle it: does the attribute have a zero whose sign means something, and does it wrap around. The answers give three classes, and using the wrong one loses information in a way that is specific and predictable rather than merely unattractive.

The range

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Reading the class off the attribute

Two questions decide which class an attribute needs, and both are answered by what the attribute measures rather than by what its display looks like.

Does zero mean anything, and does the sign? Amplitude has a zero: it separates a peak from a trough, and the difference between +0.05 and −0.05 is a difference in polarity, not a difference in size. Curvature has a zero: it separates anticlinal from synclinal bending. Envelope does not. It is a magnitude, it cannot be negative, and its smallest value is simply its smallest value.

Does it wrap? Instantaneous phase runs to +180° and continues at −180°, and those two values are adjacent measurements of almost the same thing. Dip azimuth and aberrancy azimuth do the same at 360°. Nothing else in the list wraps.

The histogram under the map is where this can be checked rather than assumed. A sequential attribute piles up somewhere in its range and tails off, with no particular significance to any interior value. An attribute with a meaningful zero is roughly symmetric about it, and the marked line falls in the middle of the mass rather than off to one side. A wrapping attribute is close to flat across its whole range, with as much data near the two ends as anywhere else, which is the signature of a quantity whose ends are joined.

Most-positive curvature is worth a second look here. It has a meaningful zero, so it belongs in the diverging group, but its histogram is not symmetric about it: the quantity is bounded below by the mean curvature and its values sit mostly above zero. The class is right and the range will need attention, which is step 3.

Three classes follow. Sequential maps run from dark to light with no interior landmark. Diverging maps have two arms either side of a neutral centre. Cyclic maps return to the same color at both ends.

2The three mismatches

The same attribute is below in one map of each class, at the same range. Changing the attribute in the panel changes all three together, so each mismatch can be produced deliberately.

 

 

 

A diverging attribute in a sequential map. Put mean curvature in Oslo. The anticlinal and synclinal sides of every fault are no longer separated by anything: they are just two different brightnesses, and which one is which has to be recovered from the color bar. The paired lineaments that make a fault recognisable on a curvature display become a single smeared band. Nothing has been hidden exactly, but the structure the attribute was computed to show has been taken apart.

A cyclic attribute in a sequential or diverging map. Put instantaneous phase in Oslo or in balance. A hard edge appears wherever the phase passes through ±180°, running across the image in lines that follow no geology. Those lines are the two ends of the colormap meeting at values that are adjacent in the data. Step 4 takes this apart.

A sequential attribute in a diverging map. Put envelope in balance. A false centre appears partway up the range: the display now asserts that some particular envelope value is a dividing line with two sides, and that value is wherever the middle of the display range happens to fall. Moving the range sliders moves it, which is the same test as module 03.

The cyclic map applied to a non-cyclic attribute is the subtlest of the three. It does not produce a hard edge, and the display often looks perfectly reasonable. What it does is render the highest and lowest values in the same color, so a bright anomaly and a dead zone become indistinguishable.

3Where the neutral point lands

Choosing a diverging map is only half of it. A diverging map has its neutral color at the middle of whatever range it is stretched across, and there is nothing to make that middle coincide with the attribute's own zero. If the range is not symmetric, it does not.

The arrow above the color bar marks where zero in the data falls. With the range symmetric it sits at the centre of the map, on the neutral color. Move either range slider with the symmetric lock off and it slides away, and the display quietly starts reporting a polarity that is not there: values that are positive appear on the negative arm, or the other way round.

Set the attribute to mean curvature, switch the symmetric lock off, and raise the bottom of the range to about 30%. The neutral color now sits on a positive curvature value. Every anticline weaker than that threshold is rendered in the synclinal arm of the colormap and reads as a syncline. The attribute volume has not changed and the color bar is labelled correctly throughout.

Most-positive curvature makes this awkward in a way worth understanding rather than working around. Its values sit mostly above zero, so a range symmetric about zero spends half its colors on data that is nearly absent, and the display looks washed out on one side. The temptation is to set the range to the data's own minimum and maximum, which puts the neutral point somewhere in the positive values. Both options are defensible and neither is automatic. What is not defensible is doing the second one without noticing.

Mean curvature does not have this problem: it is symmetric about zero by construction, and the two arms of the colormap carry roughly equal amounts of data. Where both are available, the symmetric one is the easier display to set up honestly.

The same issue arises for amplitude whenever an automatic range is computed from a window that happens to contain more peak than trough, which is common near a strong reflector. The symmetric lock is the fix, and it costs nothing on an attribute that is genuinely balanced.

4The wrap

Instantaneous phase is reported between −180° and +180°. Those two limits are the same measurement: a phase of +179° and one of −179° are two degrees apart, not 358. The place where the reported number jumps is a convention of the reporting, and nothing happens there in the data.

The rotation slider in the panel shifts where that convention puts its jump, by adding a constant to the phase and wrapping it back into the range. This changes no relationship in the data at all: every pair of samples stays the same distance apart.

In a sequential or diverging map the jump is a hard edge in the image, and rotating the wrap drags that edge across the survey. It crosses the channel, runs over the faults, and cuts through the pockmark field, and at every rotation it looks equally like a real boundary. Watching it move is the clearest available demonstration that it is not one.

In a cyclic map nothing happens. The two ends of the colormap are the same color, so the convention's jump produces no visible change, and rotating the wrap leaves the image essentially as it was. What remains is the phase structure itself, which is what the attribute was computed for.

The same applies to dip azimuth, to aberrancy azimuth, and to any attribute reported as an angle. These are common cases, and the rainbow maps still often used for azimuth are in fact cyclic in hue, which is why they do not produce a seam. What they do instead is swing strongly in lightness between hues, so particular azimuth directions come out brighter than others and appear to be preferentially developed. Bedle and Moreno-Ward (2025) show that effect on aberrancy azimuth in the Great South Basin, where the bias is toward the azimuths rendered in yellow and cyan.

A cyclic map that is also perceptually uniform, such as RomaO, gives every azimuth equal visual weight. The lineaments that stand out are then the ones that are actually strongest, rather than the ones whose direction happened to land on a bright part of the color wheel.

Why it matters

Class mismatches are the easiest of all display errors to avoid, because the correct answer is determined by the attribute and can be worked out once and then written down. They are also common, because software defaults do not know what attribute has been loaded and will apply whatever map was last used.

Each mismatch has its own failure. A sequential map on a diverging attribute discards the sign, which for curvature means discarding the distinction between an anticline and a syncline. A non-cyclic map on a wrapping attribute invents a boundary that follows a contour of the attribute and can run for kilometres. A diverging map on a sequential attribute asserts a dividing line at a value chosen by the display range.

The neutral-point problem in step 3 is the one most likely to survive into an interpretation, because nothing about the image announces it. The color bar is correct, the colors are legitimate, and the only symptom is that the boundary between the two arms of the colormap is in the wrong place. A display built with an automatic range on an attribute that is not balanced about zero will do this by default.

None of this requires the scientific colormaps. A traditional red-white-blue map used on a genuinely diverging attribute with a symmetric range is doing its job. The class has to be right first; uniformity and accessibility are then improvements on top of a correct choice rather than substitutes for one.

Exercises

  1. Sort them by histogram alone. Step through envelope, mean curvature and phase, looking only at the histogram, and decide which class each needs before reading the menu grouping.
    HintEnvelope piles up near the low end and tails off, with no interior landmark: sequential. Mean curvature is symmetric about a marked zero with two tails: diverging. Phase is close to flat from one end of its range to the other, with as much data near the limits as in the middle, which is what a wrapping quantity looks like: cyclic.
  2. Take the sign away. Put mean curvature in Oslo and then in balance, and look at one of the three main faults.
    HintIn balance the fault is a pair of lineaments, one on each arm of the colormap, and which side is up is immediate. In Oslo the pair becomes one band of changing brightness and the sign has to be recovered from the color bar for every feature separately. Nothing is hidden; the structure has been taken apart.
  3. Produce a seam and then chase it. Put phase in balance, then move the wrap rotation slider through a full turn.
    HintA hard edge sweeps across the survey. It crosses the channel and the faults and looks like a boundary at every position. Nothing in the data has changed: rotating the wrap adds a constant to every sample, so every pair of samples stays the same distance apart. Switching to RomaO makes the edge disappear entirely, and rotating then does nothing visible.
  4. Move the neutral point. Put mean curvature in balance with the symmetric lock on, note where the arrow above the color bar sits, then switch the lock off and raise the bottom of the range to 30%.
    HintThe arrow marking zero slides away from the centre of the colormap, and weak anticlines are now rendered in the synclinal arm. The color bar is still labelled correctly and there is nothing in the image to indicate the problem. This is what an automatic range does to an attribute that is not balanced about zero.
  5. Invent a boundary in a magnitude. Put envelope in balance and move the range sliders.
    HintThe neutral color falls at whatever envelope value sits in the middle of the range, and the display asserts a dividing line there with two sides to it. Moving the range moves the line, which is the module 03 test for a boundary that belongs to the display rather than to the data. Envelope has no dividing line of any kind: it is a magnitude.
  6. Find the quiet mismatch. Put envelope in RomaO and look at the strongest and the weakest parts of the display.
    HintThey are the same color, because the two ends of a cyclic map are joined. No edge appears and the image looks reasonable, which is what makes this the easiest of the mismatches to miss. A bright channel axis and a dead zone outside it cannot be told apart without the color bar.
  • Two questions fix the class: does zero mean something with a sign, and does the attribute wrap. Magnitudes are sequential, signed quantities are diverging, angles are cyclic.
  • The histogram is where the answer can be checked. A flat distribution across the whole range, with data piled at both ends, is the signature of a wrapping quantity.
  • A sequential map on a diverging attribute discards the sign. A non-cyclic map on a wrapping attribute invents a boundary at the wrap. A diverging map on a magnitude asserts a dividing line that the display range chose.
  • A cyclic map on a non-cyclic attribute produces no edge and no warning, and renders the highest and lowest values in the same color.
  • Choosing a diverging map is only half the work. Its neutral color lands at the middle of the display range, and unless that range is symmetric about zero the neutral point is not on the data's zero.
  • Most-positive curvature has a meaningful zero but an asymmetric distribution, so the symmetric range and the data's own range are both defensible and the choice has to be made deliberately.

Method

The three classes follow the categorisation in Thyng et al. (2016) and Crameri et al. (2020), and the arrangement here follows Bedle and Moreno-Ward (2025), which works the same three classes on coherence, curvature, phase and aberrancy in the Great South Basin.

Every attribute in the menu is computed from the same synthetic model, described in module 00. Phase and amplitude come from the analytic trace along the marker; mean and most-positive curvature come from a horizon picked off the deeper reflector; coherence is semblance over a 3 × 3 trace window. The grouping in the menu is by class, so that the histogram can be compared against a stated answer.

The wrap rotation adds a constant to the phase at every sample and wraps the result back into −180° to +180°. This is an exact symmetry of a cyclic quantity: every difference between samples is unchanged, and so is every relationship the attribute was computed to show. Nothing in the data distinguishes one rotation from another, which is what makes it a clean test of the display.

The arrow above the color bar is drawn at the data value zero, wherever that falls in the current display range. The colormap's own centre is at the midpoint of the range by construction. When the symmetric lock is on, the bottom and top of the range are held at equal distances from zero, so the two coincide.

The symmetric lock is offered only for attributes whose natural range straddles zero. For an attribute like envelope, which cannot be negative, there is no symmetric range to impose and the control is not shown.
