Module 00  ·  Colormaps

Why the colormap matters

Every seismic display passes the data through a colormap on its way to the screen. This module introduces the synthetic model that the rest of the set runs on, shows the seismic made from it, and then puts one attribute through two colormaps at the same value range, so that any difference between the two pictures comes from the color and nothing else.

The display

The first two are model properties and are not available from real seismic data. They are here so the attributes can be checked against them.

The value range is fixed and identical in both panels, so it is never the reason the two differ.

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises. The panel above stays put, so the sliders are always reachable.

1The earth model behind every module

A shale section containing one sinuous channel sand. The sand is thickest along the channel axis and pinches out at both margins. Three normal faults offset the whole section, a swarm of small polygonal faults sits in one part of the survey, and a field of pockmarks dimples the marker surface and dims the reflection beneath each one.

The red line on the map marks where the section is taken. Where it crosses the channel axis the sand lens is at its thickest; near a margin the top and base reflections are close together in time.

Two of the attributes in the menu — sand thickness and two-way time to the marker — are properties of the model rather than measurements. Neither could be obtained from real seismic data. They are included so that every later display can be checked against the thing it is trying to image.

2From reflectivity to a trace

Each interface has a reflection coefficient set by the contrast in acoustic impedance across it. The trace is that reflectivity series convolved with a Ricker wavelet. Every attribute in the panel above is computed from the analytic form of that trace, so envelope, instantaneous phase and instantaneous frequency all come from the same synthetic seismic the section displays.

The trace is taken at this CDP on the section line chosen in step 1.

1 The wavelet at the current peak frequency

2 The trace it produces, with the reflection coefficients drawn as spikes

Red spikes mark the reflection coefficients at the top and base of the sand. Where they are closer together than the wavelet is long, the two reflections interfere and the trace no longer resolves them.

Lowering the peak frequency lengthens the wavelet, and the two reflections merge into a single loop once the bed is thinner than the tuning thickness. Raising the sand impedance above the shale value reverses the polarity of the top reflection. Neither of these has anything to do with color, and both will be read through a colormap in every display that follows.

The attribute maps are extracted along the top of the sand, following the structure rather than at a constant time, which makes them a horizon slice rather than a time slice.

3The same numbers through two colormaps

The two maps in the panel hold identical data. The only difference between them is the colormap; the value range is the same in both and is held fixed. Any difference in what the two images communicate therefore comes from the color alone.

2 The values along one CDP line, and the same values as color

The curve and the two strips carry the same numbers. The red line on both maps marks this CDP.

Where the curve changes smoothly and a strip changes abruptly, the boundary in the image belongs to the colormap. Where the curve has a step and a strip barely changes, the colormap is spending little of its color range on that part of the data. This is what perceptual uniformity describes, and module 03 measures it.

The line under each map records the class of the colormap, whether it is perceptually uniform, and whether it stays legible under color vision deficiency. Where the class does not match the attribute, it says so.

4Features at the edge of visibility

The dashed rectangle on both maps encloses the pockmark field. Each pockmark reduces the reflection strength by a set fraction, and that fraction is under slider control here. It sets how far the pockmark anomalies stand above the values around them.

Envelope or sweetness show these most clearly.

As the dimming is reduced, the pockmarks stop being visible at different settings in the two panels. The anomaly is present in the data at every setting; the value at which it stops being visible is a property of the display. The same comparison runs with the noise slider instead, which raises the background variation rather than lowering the anomaly.

The polygonal fault swarm is the other test case. Its offsets are one to four milliseconds, a small fraction of the range spanned by the three main faults. On curvature and coherence those small offsets occupy a narrow band near the middle of the color range, so whether they are visible depends on how the colormap behaves in its middle rather than at its ends.

Mean curvature with no noise is the clearest view of the swarm: the faults appear as paired positive and negative lineaments. Raising the noise moves the horizon pick from trace to trace, and the swarm disappears into the texture that produces, while the three main faults stay put.

Why it matters

An interpretation is made from what is visible on a screen. Two interpreters working the same volume with different color settings are not looking at the same thing, and neither of them has any indication of that from the display.

Three consequences follow, and the modules that come after this one take them in turn.

A detection limit is a property of a display, not of a data set. The noise level at which the pockmarks stop being visible in step 4 differs between the two panels, often by a factor of two. A statement that an attribute can or cannot resolve a feature is incomplete without the colormap and the value range that went with it.

Boundaries can be manufactured. A colormap that is not perceptually uniform changes color quickly at some values and slowly at others. The quick changes read as edges. They fall at values fixed by the color table, which means they appear in the same place on every display made with that table, across every survey, which is exactly what makes them convincing.

Not everyone sees the same image. Color vision deficiencies affect roughly 4%–8% of men and up to about 2% of women (Birch, 2012). A display that carries its information in a red-green distinction withholds that information from those interpreters, and does so silently.

None of this is an argument that rainbow displays are always wrong or that scientific colormaps are always right. It is an argument that the colormap is part of the measurement and should be recorded and chosen like one.

Exercises

  1. Find what each colormap favors. Set the attribute to envelope, the upper colormap to rainbow and the lower to Oslo. Find one feature easier to locate in the rainbow display, and one easier to locate in Oslo.
    HintThe channel axis holds the highest envelope values and is easy to pick out in the rainbow map, because the top of that colormap turns red against a green and yellow background. The channel margins, where the envelope changes gradually as the bed thins, are easier to follow in Oslo: its lightness changes steadily through that part of the range, while the rainbow holds nearly constant lightness across its green and yellow section.
  2. Watch the middle of the range. Set the attribute to coherence with both colormaps on grayscale and note which faults are visible. Change the upper colormap to rainbow and compare the polygonal fault swarm.
    HintThe polygonal faults produce coherence values just below one, close to the background. Grayscale renders that as a small step in brightness, which the eye detects readily because spatial detail is carried mainly by luminance. The rainbow puts a hue change in the middle of its range, which draws attention to one fixed value rather than to the discontinuities, and the small offsets become harder to trace.
  3. Break a cyclic attribute. Set the attribute to instantaneous phase with the cyclic rainbow above and RomaO below. Look at the boundary between the deep blue and the deep red in each.
    HintPhase wraps from +180° to −180°, and those two values are adjacent in the data. A cyclic colormap returns to the same color at both ends, so the wrap produces no edge. The cyclic rainbow is also cyclic in hue, but its lightness swings strongly between hues, so bright bands appear at particular phase values and read as structure that is not in the data.
  4. Measure a detection limit twice. Set the noise to zero and the pockmark dimming to 10%. Raise the noise until the pockmarks are no longer visible in the upper panel, then look at the lower panel at that same noise level.
    HintThe noise level at which the pockmarks disappear differs between the two displays, often by a factor of two or more. What is being measured is not a property of the data but of the combination of data, colormap and value range. Reporting a detection limit therefore requires stating the display settings as well.
  5. Reverse the polarity. Set the sand impedance to 7.5, above the shale value of 6.5, and watch the amplitude map and the trace in step 2.
    HintThe top of the sand becomes a positive reflection coefficient and the base becomes negative, reversing the loop. On an amplitude map with a diverging colormap the channel moves from one arm of the colormap to the other. On the envelope, which discards the sign, the channel looks much as it did before.
  6. Compare the two curvature attributes. With the noise at zero, look at most-positive curvature and then mean curvature, both in balance.
    HintMost-positive curvature is bounded below by the mean curvature, so on a noisy surface its values sit mostly above zero and the neutral point of a diverging colormap no longer falls in the middle of the data. Mean curvature is symmetric about zero and shows the faults as paired positive and negative lineaments. The colormap is the same in both; what differs is whether the attribute's own distribution is centered on the colormap's neutral point.

Key points

  • A display is the data, the value range and the colormap together. Two of the three can be held fixed while the third is changed, and that is how every comparison in this module set is made.
  • The eye resolves fine spatial detail mainly through changes in lightness. A colormap whose lightness is flat over part of its range shows little detail in that part of the data, whatever its hues are doing.
  • A colormap that is not perceptually uniform places boundaries in the image at positions set by the color table rather than by the data.
  • The level at which a feature stops being visible depends on the display as well as on the data, so a detection limit quoted without the display settings is incomplete.
  • The attributes used here fall into three classes: those running from low to high with no natural center, those with a meaningful zero and a sign, and those that wrap. Module 04 works through the colormap class that belongs to each.

Method

The model is a 200 × 160 map of a shale section containing one sinuous channel sand, built in assets/colormodel.js. Outside the channel the marker is a single interface, shale over a levee unit. Inside it, the upper part of that unit is sand, so the marker becomes a pair of reflections whose separation is set by the sand thickness. A deeper reflector 118 ms below carries the structure without the pockmarks.

Reflectivity is convolved with a Ricker wavelet. Rather than building a full volume and transforming it, the analytic trace is evaluated directly at whatever time is asked for, using a tabulated wavelet and its quadrature. Amplitude is the real part, envelope the magnitude, phase the angle, and instantaneous frequency the rate of change of phase. Coherence is semblance over a 3 × 3 trace window and a seven-sample vertical window, computed without dip steering, so a fault offset shows as a waveform mismatch. Curvature is computed from a horizon picked as the strongest envelope within ±10 ms of the deeper reflector, refined by a parabola, which is why noise and bandwidth move it.

Noise is three band-limited components with independent, spatially correlated phases, scaled as a fraction of a strong reflection. Correlating it over a few traces is what makes it behave like seismic noise rather than like speckle.

Display ranges are fixed constants in Synthetic.RANGES. Moving a slider therefore changes the picture and not the scale, which matters because an axis that rescales under the data is not noticed while a bar or a curve that moves is. Module 06 is where the ranges come under the student's control.
