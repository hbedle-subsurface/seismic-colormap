Four decisions and one record. The order matters more than any single choice.

1Class before colors

The first decision is not which colormap looks best. It is which class the attribute needs, and the attribute decides that, not the interpreter. Getting it wrong cannot be repaired by any later choice.

Two questions, from module 04. Does zero mean something, with a sign that carries information? Does the attribute wrap around? Magnitudes take a sequential map, signed quantities a diverging one, angles a cyclic one. The readout in the panel says which class the current attribute needs and which class the current colormap is, and whether they agree.

Within the right class, the second decision is whether the lightness runs one way from end to end. That is what carries the fine detail, from module 02, and what survives color vision deficiency, from module 05. Almost everything else about a colormap is a matter of taste; this is not.

Grayscale belongs in that first row and is worth more than its reputation. It measured 1.8 on the spacing test in module 03, better than viridis, and it is untouched by every form of color vision deficiency. If a display is about where the edges are, it is often the right answer and it is almost never the wrong one.

2The range

The range changes a display at least as much as the colormap does, and it is the part left to a default most often. The histogram in the panel has the two limits marked on it.

Set it from the distribution rather than from the extremes. A 2–98% clip is a reasonable starting point for a clean attribute and 5–95% for a noisy one, and the difference between those two matters far less than the difference between either of them and using the raw minimum and maximum. The readout says how many of the colormap's levels are reaching the middle half of the data, which is the number worth watching as the limits move.

If the attribute has a meaningful zero, lock the range symmetric. Otherwise the neutral color of a diverging map lands wherever the middle of the range happens to fall, and the display starts reporting a sign that is not there.

Reach for a stretch only after the clip. A gamma spreads the colors over one end of the range and undoes the even spacing that module 03 measured, which is a reasonable trade when it is deliberate. A logarithmic stretch is the right answer for an attribute that genuinely spans orders of magnitude, and should be stated on the color bar when it is used.

A strong gamma is usually a sign that something earlier is wrong: the clip, the attribute, or the question. Try the clip first and the stretch second, and if a gamma above about two is needed to make features appear, go back and look at why.

3Run the checks

Four checks, none of which takes more than a few seconds, and each of which catches a failure that is invisible from inside the finished display.

Move the range and see what moves with it. Features that hold still belong to the data. Features that slide belong to the colormap. This is the module 03 test and it is the single most useful thing in this module set.

Compare against grayscale. Switch the colormap to grayscale and back. Anything visible in grayscale and not in the colored version was being carried by hue, which cannot resolve it. Anything visible in the colored version and not in grayscale deserves a second look.

Simulate color vision deficiency. The deutan and protan buttons, and the no-color button, which is the strictest test there is: what survives it is carried by lightness. The readout gives the smallest step that survives deutan simulation, and a number near zero means a stretch of the range has become one flat color.

Look at the section. The Section button at the top of the panel swaps the map for a vertical slice. An attribute that looks convincing in map view and incoherent in section is usually being extracted along the wrong surface, or is responding to something other than what it is supposed to measure. The inline direction crosses all three faults; the crossline crosses the channel.

Two more worth adding when they apply. If the display is corendered, toggle the second attribute off and on: anything that appears and disappears with it belongs to it. If the figure is going to print, check the dark end, because the ink limit takes the saturated colors first.

4Write it down

A figure carries its colormap visibly and its range as two numbers nobody reads. Every other decision is invisible. The text below is generated from the current settings and is what a caption needs to make the display reproducible.

It is longer than most captions and shorter than most figures deserve. The parts that matter are the ones a reader cannot recover from the image: the range, the stretch, the second attribute and what it was doing, and any threshold.

A blend needs more again. Three attributes, three ranges, three gains and the assignment of attributes to channels is eight pieces of information, from module 09, and a blend without them cannot be reproduced by anyone including the person who made it.

The habit worth forming is not writing long captions. It is noticing which of your decisions are invisible in the result. Everything in the generated text is a decision that was made, and every one of them could have been made differently and produced a different figure from the same data.

That is the end of the module set. Every module has made the same argument about a different control: the display is chosen, it can be chosen badly, and the image carries no record of which choices were made.

Why it matters

Interpretation is a judgement made from what is visible. Everything in these modules follows from that. A display that hides a feature produces an interpretation without it; a display that invents a boundary produces one with it; and neither leaves any sign on the image.

The practical version is short enough to remember. Match the class to the attribute. Let lightness carry the detail. Set the range from the distribution. Check by moving something and seeing what moves with it. Write down what you did.

There is a second reason, which is that a figure is rarely made only for the person making it. It goes into a report, onto a screen in a meeting, past a colleague who has ten seconds for it. Whatever the display hides is hidden from all of them, and whatever it invents is something they will discuss. Recording the settings is what lets somebody else check the figure instead of taking it on trust, which is the standard applied to every other step of the work.

None of this requires new software or new colormaps. Most of it is available in whatever package is already being used, and the parts that are not — a simulator, a percentile clip — take minutes to arrange. What it requires is treating the display as a choice rather than as a default, which is the only part that is genuinely a change in habit.

The counterargument deserves a hearing. Interpreters have worked productively in rainbow colormaps for forty years, and Ware et al. (2023) make a reasonable case that the rainbow's bad reputation is partly overstated: its high number of distinguishable colors is genuinely useful for some tasks, and an experienced reader who knows a colormap's quirks can work around them. The reply is that the workarounds are individual and unwritten, and that a display which needs them is doing less work than it could. Where a rainbow is chosen deliberately, for a reason, that is a considered decision. Where it is the default, it is not a decision at all.

Exercises

  1. Build a coherence display from nothing. Set the attribute to coherence and work through the six control groups in order, then read the record in step 4.
    HintCoherence is a magnitude, so sequential; grayscale or Oslo. It is heavily skewed toward one, so a percentile clip matters more than usual, and the interesting variation is in the top few per cent of the range. No symmetric lock, because there is no zero. It is the attribute least in need of a second attribute corendered onto it, because it is usually the one being corendered onto something else.
  2. Build the same display badly, on purpose. Put coherence in a cyclic map at the full range with a gamma of 0.4.
    HintThe highest and lowest values come out the same color, the range is being held open by a handful of samples, and the stretch has undone whatever even spacing the map had. The image still looks like a competent display, which is the problem this module set exists to address.
  3. Find an attribute that needs the symmetric lock. Try mean curvature and amplitude with the lock off and a 5–95% clip, then with it on.
    HintBoth are signed, and a percentile clip on either is very unlikely to come out symmetric about zero on its own. With the lock off the neutral color of a diverging map lands away from zero and the display reports a sign that is not there.
  4. Run the grayscale check on a rainbow display. Put envelope in the rainbow at a 2–98% clip, then switch to grayscale and back.
    HintThe channel margins and the pockmark field are easier to follow in grayscale, because they are low-contrast fine detail and that is carried by lightness. The rainbow is better at making the channel axis obvious, which is the one feature that needed no help.
  5. Use the section to catch something. Set the attribute to phase and switch to the section view, inline.
    HintIn section the phase is coherent along the reflections and noise everywhere between them, which is not visible in map view because the map is extracted along the marker. It is the argument for masking a phase display by reflection strength, from module 08.
  6. Write a caption for a display you would defend. Build anything you like, read the record, and decide which lines you would have thought to write unprompted.
    HintMost people write the attribute and the colormap. Most people do not write the range, the stretch or the corendering, and those are exactly the ones a reader cannot recover from the picture.
  7. Build a display, copy the record, and hand only the record to someone else. Ask them to reproduce the figure and note everything they have to ask you.
    HintUsually the level and the corendering strength, because those are the two that leave no trace in the image. If a colleague cannot rebuild the figure from what you wrote down, they cannot check it, and the settings are part of the work in the same way the processing parameters are.
  8. Take a figure you have already made for a talk or a report and run the four checks from step 3 on it.
    HintThe common outcomes are a boundary that moves with the range, a feature that is stronger in grayscale than in the colormap that was used, and a display whose information is in a red-to-green distinction. None of these means the interpretation was wrong. They mean the figure was making a weaker case than the data supports.
  • Class first. The attribute decides it, and no later choice repairs getting it wrong.
  • Within the class, pick a colormap whose lightness runs one way. That is what carries fine detail and what survives color vision deficiency.
  • Set the range from the distribution, not from the extremes, and lock it symmetric when the attribute has a meaningful zero.
  • Clip before stretching. A gamma above about two usually means something earlier needs revisiting.
  • Four checks: move the range and see what moves; compare against grayscale; simulate color vision deficiency; look at a section.
  • Write down what a reader cannot recover from the image: the range, the stretch, the second attribute and its threshold. A blend needs eight pieces of information.
  • A figure records its attribute and its colormap. It does not record the range, the stretch, the level or the corendering, so those have to be written down.

Method

This module reuses the machinery of the ones before it. The range and the percentile presets are from module 06, the stretch is applied by warping a copy of the colormap as it is there, the levels are the quantization of module 07, the corendering is the linear-light compositing of module 08, and the simulation is the Machado model used in module 05. Nothing new is computed; the point of the module is that all of it is one display and one set of decisions.

The record is generated from the current settings. It reports the attribute, the colormap with its source, the class agreement, the range with both the values and the percentiles they correspond to, the stretch, the number of levels, any corendered attribute with its method and strength, and the model parameters that produced the synthetic. On real data the last line would be the survey, the processing product and the extraction surface instead.

The order of the six control groups is the order the decisions are worth making in, and it is not arbitrary. The class constrains the colormap; the colormap and the range together decide how much of the data is separable; the stretch only makes sense once the range is fixed; and a second attribute should be added to a display that already works rather than used to rescue one that does not.
