Module 07  ·  Colormaps

Continuous and discrete color

Module 03 treated banding as a fault, because the bands came from a colormap that nobody chose to put there. Bands deliberately placed at known values are a different object. They turn a display that can only be compared into one that can be read, at the price of everything inside each band. This module is about when that trade is worth making, and about the output that has no continuous values to begin with.

The data

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Taking the levels away

The slider reduces the colormap from its full 256 levels down to two. Nothing else changes: the attribute, the range and the colors along the map are all as they were.

Coming down from 256, the first thing to notice is how far it can go before anything is visible. At 64 levels the display is indistinguishable from continuous. At 32 the smoothest gradients begin to show faint steps. At 16 the bands are plain, and below 8 the display has become a map of which bin each sample fell in.

What is being lost is variation within each band. Two samples in the same bin are rendered identically however far apart they are inside it, and the width of one bin, in the attribute's own units, is in the readout. At 8 levels on envelope that width is larger than the difference between the channel margin and the background, which is why the margin turns from a gradient into an edge.

These bands are the same object as the false boundaries in module 03, produced deliberately rather than by accident. The difference is that their positions are known, stated on the color bar, and the same for every reader. A boundary a reader can see the reason for is a different thing from one that looks like geology.

The histogram in the panel marks the bin edges, which is where the steps in the image come from. On an attribute whose distribution is lopsided, most of the samples end up in one or two bins and the rest are nearly empty, and that is the subject of step 3.

2What steps are for

A continuous display supports comparison. It is easy to see that one part of a map is higher than another, and close to impossible to say by how much, or to read a value off it and get the same answer as a colleague.

A stepped display trades that around. Each color names a range of values rather than a value, and that name is stated on the color bar, so two readers examining the same feature will place it in the same bin. Nothing inside the bin can be recovered, and nothing inside the bin has to be argued about.

Three situations where the trade is usually worth making.

A threshold that means something. Coherence below some value is where an interpreter starts picking faults. Setting the levels so that a bin edge falls at that value makes the display show directly where the criterion is met, instead of showing a gradient and leaving the threshold to the eye. The same argument applies to any attribute with a cutoff attached to it.

Reading numbers off a map. A thickness map that will be used to estimate a volume, or a time-structure map that will be used to pick a contour, is being read rather than compared. Steps at stated intervals are the map-making convention for exactly this reason, and a contoured map is the limiting case of a stepped one.

Counting. Three or four bands can be counted across an image and their widths compared; a continuous gradient cannot. Where the question is how far an anomaly extends rather than how strong it is, a small number of levels answers it better.

Against those, the case for keeping a display continuous is that the reader is looking for features rather than values, which is most of interpretation, and that any bin edge is a boundary drawn through data that has nothing at that value. Two-way time to the marker is the attribute in the menu where the comparison is clearest, because it is a smooth structural surface: continuous shows the shape, stepped shows the contours, and neither is a substitute for the other.

3Where the edges go

Choosing how many bins is the smaller decision. Choosing where their edges fall is the larger one, and it has the same character as the range choice in module 06: there is no neutral option, including the default.

Equal value divides the range into bins of the same width. It is the option almost every piece of software offers first, and it is right when the bin edges are supposed to be round numbers the reader can use. It has a specific failure: on a lopsided attribute nearly all the samples land in one or two bins and the other colors are spent on data that barely exists. The readout gives the share of the survey in the largest and smallest bins, and at eight equal-value levels the numbers on this model are stark. Envelope puts 68% of the survey in one bin and 0.6% in the emptiest. Sweetness puts 74% in one bin. Coherence puts 88% in one bin and 0.04% in the emptiest, which is to say that seven of its eight colors are describing a fortieth of the data between them.

Two-way time to the marker is the exception and shows what the option is good at: 22% in the largest bin and 2.5% in the smallest. A smooth structural surface spreads itself fairly evenly across its own range, which is why contour intervals of equal value have been the convention for structure maps for as long as there have been structure maps.

Equal population divides the survey so that each bin holds the same number of samples, which is the same idea as the percentile clip of module 06 applied inside the range rather than at its ends. Every color then does the same amount of work, and the display separates the bulk of the data rather than the tails. Its cost is that the bin edges are no longer round numbers and no longer evenly spaced, so the color bar must be labelled with the actual values or it will be misread. A reader who assumes the bins are evenly spaced will conclude that the attribute varies more smoothly than it does.

The choice follows from what the display is for. Equal value keeps the arithmetic honest and is right when the numbers are going to be used. Equal population keeps the picture informative and is right when the question is where things are.

This is the same decision as the percentile clip in module 06, taken inside the range rather than at its ends. A percentile clip sets the two outermost edges from the distribution; equal population sets all of them that way.

One combination is worth avoiding. Equal population with a diverging colormap moves the neutral color off the attribute's zero, for the reason set out in module 04: the middle of the display is now the median of the data rather than zero, and unless the attribute happens to be symmetric those are different values.

4Output with no order

Everything so far has quantized a continuous attribute, so the bins retain an order even after the values inside them are discarded. Classification output does not work that way. The classes produced by an unsupervised method are labels, and the numbers attached to them are arbitrary: class 3 is not between class 2 and class 4 in any sense.

The sequential version asserts something false. It says that the classes run from low to high, that two classes rendered in similar tones are similar to each other, and that the dark end and the light end are opposite extremes of something. None of that is in the output. The labels could be permuted without changing the classification in any way, and the sequential display would then tell a completely different story about the same result.

The categorical version makes no claim beyond difference. Its colors are chosen to be distinguishable from each other and to sit at similar lightness, so no class looks more important than any other. That is the whole requirement, and it is why hue — which module 02 found to be poor at carrying order and detail — is exactly right here.

Two practical points follow.

The permutation test. If relabelling the classes would change what the display appears to say, the display is asserting an order that the method did not produce. This is worth running mentally on any classification figure.

Some classification output does have an order. A self-organizing map lays its classes out on a grid so that neighbors are similar, and a colormap that follows that grid is carrying real information rather than inventing it. The distinction is whether the ordering came from the method or from the display, and it has to be checked rather than assumed.

Why it matters

Stepped displays are unusual in seismic attribute work and common in the disciplines that read maps for numbers. Part of that is convention and part of it is that most attribute interpretation is a search for features rather than a measurement of values, where continuous color is the better tool.

The cases that go wrong are the ones in between. An attribute with a threshold attached to it, a thickness map about to be turned into a volume, a probability cube where the question is which parts exceed a cutoff: all of these are being read rather than compared, and a continuous display makes that reading unreliable in a way nobody notices, because a reader will always produce an answer.

The classification case is more serious, because it is an assertion rather than an imprecision. A sequential colormap applied to facies output tells the reader that the classes are ordered. Readers believe it, because that is what a sequential colormap means everywhere else, and they will describe the result in terms of a trend that does not exist. Permuting the labels and redrawing the figure is a ten-second check that settles it.

Where a stepped display is used, the color bar has to carry the actual bin edges. A stepped bar with evenly spaced labels over unevenly spaced bins is worse than no bar at all, because it is specific and wrong.

Exercises

  1. Find where banding starts. With envelope in Oslo, bring the levels down from continuous and note the first setting where the steps are visible, then the first where they are distracting.
    HintVisibility usually begins somewhere around 32 levels and the display becomes a set of bands by 16. The exact point depends on the smoothness of the attribute and on the screen, and it is worth noticing that this is the same threshold that decides whether a badly designed 256-level colormap looks banded: what matters is the size of the step in appearance, not the number of entries.
  2. Put an edge on a threshold. Put the attribute on coherence at eight levels and look at where the bin edges fall relative to the fault traces.
    HintOne of the edges falls partway down the flank of each fault, and the fault then reads as a band of fixed width rather than as a gradient. Whether that is useful depends on whether the edge is at a value that means something. If it is, the display is showing where the criterion is met; if not, it is drawing a contour through nothing.
  3. Watch a lopsided attribute waste its colors. Put the attribute on sweetness at eight equal-value levels and read the largest and smallest bin shares.
    HintOne bin holds most of the survey and several hold almost none, because sweetness has a long tail toward high values. Switching to equal population evens them out and the display separates the bulk of the data, at the cost of bin edges that are no longer round numbers and no longer evenly spaced.
  4. Break a diverging map with equal population. Put mean curvature in balance at eight levels and switch between the two binning modes.
    HintWith equal population the neutral color sits at the median of the data rather than at zero, so the boundary between the two arms of the colormap is no longer the boundary between anticlinal and synclinal. This is the module 04 problem arriving through a different door.
  5. Permute the classes. On step 4, compare the two classification displays, then imagine the class numbers being shuffled.
    HintThe categorical version would look different and say the same thing. The sequential version would look different and say something different, because it reads the label numbers as magnitudes. Since the labels are arbitrary, any story the sequential display tells about a trend is a story about the numbering.
  6. Change the number of classes. On step 4, move the classes slider and watch which features survive.
    HintThe channel and the background separate at three classes. The channel margin appears as its own class somewhere around five, and beyond six the additional classes mostly subdivide the background. The number of classes is a modelling choice, and the display cannot tell the reader which of the classes are real distinctions.
  • Reducing a colormap to a small number of levels discards variation inside each bin and produces bands. The bands are the same object as the false boundaries of module 03, with the difference that their positions are chosen, stated and the same for every reader.
  • Continuous color supports comparison. Stepped color supports reading a value and agreeing about it. Most attribute interpretation wants the first, and anything with a threshold or a number attached wants the second.
  • Equal-value bins keep the arithmetic simple and waste colors on a lopsided attribute. Equal-population bins spend every color usefully and require the bar to be labelled with the real edges.
  • Equal-population binning moves the neutral point of a diverging colormap off zero, for the same reason an asymmetric range does.
  • Classification output has no order. A sequential colormap applied to it asserts one, and permuting the labels would change what the figure appears to say.
  • Categorical color is distinct hues at similar lightness, which claims difference and nothing else.

Method

Levels are applied by rounding the normalized value to the nearest of the chosen number of steps before the colormap is looked up, which is the same operation the color bar shows. The top of the slider leaves the full 256-level table in place.

Equal-population binning is done by replacing each value with its rank in the sorted data before quantizing, so the bins hold equal counts by construction. The color bar is then labelled with the actual values at the bin edges, which are recovered from the same sorted array. Displaying an equal-population map against evenly spaced labels would misstate the attribute, so the labels move instead of the colors.

The classification in step 4 is k-means on two attributes, envelope and coherence, each standardized to zero mean and unit standard deviation so that neither dominates through its units. Initial centers are placed at evenly spaced quantiles of the first attribute and the algorithm is run to convergence or twenty iterations, from a fixed starting point so the result does not change between visits. The class labels are then in whatever order the initialisation produced, which is exactly the point being made: they carry no meaning.

This is a deliberately plain method, chosen because it is easy to state. Self-organizing maps and generative topographic mapping, which are the usual tools for this in seismic work, differ in one respect that matters here: they place their classes on a grid so that neighboring classes are similar, so a colormap that follows the grid carries real information. k-means produces no such structure.
