Corendering two attributes

Two attributes in one image is the normal case in interpretation, and the usual result is that neither can be read. The three dimensions of module 02 are what makes it work: one attribute takes the hue, another takes the lightness, and a third option uses one attribute to decide where the other is shown at all.

The two attributes

The second attribute is not given colors of its own. It is given one of the other dimensions of the display.

How they are combined

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1One image, two attributes

The obvious way to show two attributes at once is to give each a colormap and lay one over the other at half opacity. It does not work: both colormaps are trying to use the same three dimensions of the display, and the result is a mixture that reports neither.

What does work follows from module 02. The display has a lightness, a chroma and a hue, and they are separable. Give one attribute the hue and let it keep its colormap. Give the other a dimension the first is not using. The four buttons in the panel are the options.

MethodWhat the second attribute doesWhere it suits
First onlyNothing; the reference displayAlways worth looking at first
LightnessDarkens the image where it is lowAn edge attribute over anything: coherence over amplitude, energy or sweetness
SaturationDrains the color toward gray where it is lowWhere the first attribute is only meaningful in part of the survey
TransparencyDecides where the first is shown at allAn attribute with a companion magnitude: azimuth with aberrancy strength, phase with reflection strength

The first attribute keeps its own color bar and remains readable against it. The second does not get a color bar in the usual sense, because it has not been given colors: its scale is the second strip in the panel, which shows what it does to the image rather than what color it is.

The constraint that decides which method to use is the one from module 02. Lightness carries fine detail, so the attribute with fine detail should be the one taking the lightness. On a coherence-over-envelope display that is coherence, whose content is thin fault traces, while the envelope varies smoothly. Putting them the other way round wastes the dimension that can resolve the faults on an attribute that has no fine structure to show.

2Lightness from the second attribute

The standard corendered display in seismic interpretation is an amplitude-like attribute in color with a coherence attribute darkening it. The strength slider sets how much of the lightness the second attribute is allowed to take.

At zero strength the display is the first attribute alone. Raising it, the fault traces and the channel edges appear as dark lineaments cut into the color, and they appear as lineaments rather than as a wash because coherence is low along thin curved features and high everywhere else.

Two things go wrong at high strength, and both are visible in the readouts.

The first attribute loses its range. Its colors are multiplied down wherever coherence is less than perfect, which on real data is most of the survey. The readout gives the lightness the first attribute still has to work with where the second is at its lowest: Oslo covers L* 2 to 91 unmodulated, and at 80% strength that falls to L* 1 to 52 in the worst-affected areas. The color bar stops describing the image, because a value that should be rendered at the light end of the colormap is rendered somewhere below it, by an amount the reader cannot recover.

The overall image gets darker rather than flatter, which is worth being clear about. Its total lightness range actually widens, because the modulation pushes some cells to black while the unmodulated cells stay where they were. What has been compressed is the range available to the first attribute at any given value of the second, and that compression is invisible in the finished picture.

The colormap's own dark end disappears. A colormap that reaches near-black at one end has nothing left to darken there. Oslo starts at L* 2, so a low envelope in a coherent area and a moderate envelope in an incoherent one are both rendered close to black and cannot be told apart. Viridis and batlow start at L* 16 and 14, which is a little room but not much.

That second problem is the argument for corendering with a colormap that stops well short of black. Reserving the bottom of the lightness scale so a second attribute can use it is a real design consideration, and it is one of the reasons a corendered display often wants a different colormap from the single-attribute display of the same data.

A strength somewhere between 50% and 80% is where most displays of this kind end up, and the right value depends on whether the reader is being asked to read the first attribute's values or only to see where its features are. If the values matter, the strength should be low and the second attribute should probably be a separate display.

3Showing it only where it means something

Some attributes are undefined, unstable or meaningless over part of a survey rather than everywhere. Instantaneous phase is the clearest case: it is a well-determined number where a reflection is strong and close to noise where the trace is weak, and a phase display renders both with equal confidence.

The transparency method uses the second attribute to decide where the first is shown. Where the second is above the threshold the first appears in full color; where it is below, the display falls back to a neutral gray. The taper sets how abruptly that happens.

Set the first attribute to phase in RomaO and the second to envelope. The phase structure now appears only along the marker and the channel, where there is a reflection to measure it from, and the weak areas are quiet gray rather than a field of confident color that means nothing.

This is the arrangement used for aberrancy in Bedle and Moreno-Ward (2025): aberrancy azimuth in a cyclic colormap, masked by aberrancy magnitude, so that the lineation directions are shown only where there is a lineation. It applies to the same pairing generally — dip azimuth masked by dip magnitude, phase masked by envelope, any angle masked by the strength of the thing whose angle it is.

The threshold is a claim about the data and should be treated like the display range in module 06: reported, and chosen for a reason. A mask threshold set until the picture looks right is a decision about which parts of the survey the reader gets to see, made without a stated criterion.

The taper is worth setting to zero once, to see why it is normally not. A hard threshold puts a contour of the second attribute into the image as a visible edge, and that edge is a boundary in the display rather than in the data, in exactly the sense of module 03. A taper of ten or twenty per cent removes it without changing what is being shown.

4What it costs

A corendered display is a compromise, and it is worth being specific about what has been given up, because the image looks complete either way.

Neither attribute remains quantitative. The first has had its lightness modified by the second, so its color bar no longer describes the image. The second has no color bar at all. A corendered display supports statements about where things are and not about how large they are, and any number taken off one should be taken off a single-attribute display instead.

The cues can collide. Two attributes that vary together produce an image where the contributions cannot be separated. Envelope and sweetness are largely the same information in this model, and corendering them produces a display that looks informative and contains one attribute's worth of content. The check is to switch the method to first only and back.

Color vision deficiency hits corendered displays harder. The simulation buttons are in the panel. A single-attribute display that fails under deutan simulation is missing information; a corendered one is missing information from one layer while the other layer continues to look authoritative, and the result is harder to recognise as broken. Corendering with a colormap whose hue is doing the work, such as a rainbow, produces exactly this.

Three checks make the compromise safe enough to live with.

  • Look at each attribute alone first. Anything in the corendered display that was not in one of the two single displays is an artefact of the combination.
  • Toggle the second attribute off and on. A feature that appears and disappears with it belongs to it; a feature that persists belongs to the first.
  • Run the simulation. If the first attribute's contribution disappears under deutan simulation while the second's remains, the display is carrying its primary information in hue.

None of this argues against corendering. It is the only practical way to look at an edge attribute and an amplitude attribute together, and interpreters do it constantly for good reason. It argues for treating the combined image as a reconnaissance display and going back to the separate ones before measuring anything.

Why it matters

Multiattribute display is where most of the practical difficulty in seismic visualization sits, and it is the subject of Marfurt (2015), which remains the reference treatment. The reason it is difficult is that the display has three dimensions and no more, and every attribute added has to be given one of them or squeezed alongside something else.

The common failure is not a bad combination but an unrecorded one. A corendered figure shows a color bar for one attribute and says nothing about the second: which attribute it was, what range it covered, what it was doing to the image, and what threshold was applied. A reader cannot reconstruct any of that from the picture, and frequently neither can the person who made it, six months later.

The second failure is treating a corendered display as quantitative. It is the natural thing to do, because one attribute still has its color bar, and that color bar is wrong for the image in front of it as soon as any modulation is applied.

Both are avoidable by writing down what was combined and how. Two attribute names, two ranges, the method and any threshold: five pieces of information that make the figure reproducible, and which are almost never present.

Exercises

  1. Put the detail in the right dimension. With envelope in color and coherence on lightness, note how the faults appear. Then swap them: coherence in color, envelope on lightness.
    HintCoherence on the lightness renders the fault traces as thin dark lineaments, because lightness resolves fine detail. With the two swapped, the faults are still present but softer, and the envelope is doing nothing useful with a dimension that could have resolved them. The attribute with the fine structure should have the lightness.
  2. Lose the dark end. With envelope in Oslo and coherence on lightness, take the strength to 100% and look at the low-envelope areas outside the channel.
    HintThey are black, and so are the low-coherence areas, and the two are no longer distinguishable. Oslo runs down to near-black, so there is nothing left for the second attribute to take. A colormap that stops short of black keeps the bottom of the lightness scale available, which is what makes it a better choice for corendering.
  3. Mask a phase display. Set phase in RomaO with no second attribute, then turn on transparency with envelope as the mask.
    HintUnmasked, the phase display covers the whole survey with equal conviction, including the areas where there is barely a reflection and the phase is measuring noise. Masked, it appears along the marker and the channel and nowhere else. Nothing has been removed from the data; the display has stopped asserting a measurement where there was no signal to measure.
  4. Put a contour in by accident. With the transparency method running, set the taper to zero and look at the boundary of the masked region.
    HintA hard edge appears, following a contour of the second attribute. It is a boundary in the display, in the module 03 sense, and it is easy to read as a geological edge because it is smooth and continuous. A taper of ten or twenty per cent removes it while showing the same information.
  5. Corender an attribute with itself. Set both to envelope, or one to envelope and the other to sweetness, and switch the method on and off.
    HintThe display changes and gains nothing, because the two attributes carry nearly the same information in this model. A corendered image always looks richer than a single one, and that appearance is no evidence that a second independent thing is being shown.
  6. Check it under simulation. Put envelope in the rainbow with coherence on lightness, then switch the simulation to deutan.
    HintThe coherence layer survives, because it is working in lightness. The envelope layer loses its upper range, because the rainbow carries that in hue. The result still looks like a competent corendered display while one of its two layers has stopped working, which is harder to notice than a single-attribute display failing outright.
  • Two colormaps laid over each other compete for the same three dimensions. Corendering works by giving each attribute a different dimension.
  • The attribute with the fine detail should take the lightness, because lightness is what resolves fine detail.
  • Modulating lightness eats into the first attribute's range, and its color bar stops describing the image as soon as any modulation is applied.
  • A colormap that reaches black has nothing left to darken. Maps that stop short of black leave the bottom of the lightness scale free for a second attribute.
  • Transparency masking is the right method when the second attribute says where the first is meaningful: azimuth with magnitude, phase with reflection strength. The threshold is a claim about the data and belongs in the caption.
  • A hard mask edge is a contour of the second attribute drawn into the image. A taper removes it.
  • Corendered displays are for reconnaissance. Numbers come off single-attribute displays.

Method

The first attribute is looked up in its colormap in the usual way. The second is normalized to its own range across the survey and then applied, in linear light rather than in the stored sRGB values, so that half-strength modulation removes half the light rather than half the encoded number.

Lightness multiplies each channel by a factor running from one down to one minus the strength as the second attribute falls from its maximum to its minimum.

Saturation moves each color toward the neutral gray of the same luminance, by the same fraction, so the lightness of the image is unchanged and only the chroma falls.

Transparency computes an opacity from the second attribute: zero below the threshold, one above the threshold plus the taper, and a smooth ramp between. The color is then composited over a mid gray at that opacity. A gray background is used rather than white or black so that the muted areas do not read as either a high or a low value of the first attribute.

The color vision simulation is applied last, to the finished composite, which is the correct order: it is a property of the reader rather than of either attribute, and simulating the layers separately would not reproduce what happens to the combination.

The muted percentage counts cells whose opacity is below a tenth. The range readout takes the two ends of the colormap, puts them through the same modulation at the second attribute's minimum, and reports the lightness they land at. That is the range the first attribute has left in the worst-affected parts of the survey, which is the quantity that matters and is not the same as the lightness range of the finished image.
