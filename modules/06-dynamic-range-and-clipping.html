Dynamic range and clipping

Every module so far has held the value range fixed so that only the colormap was in question. It is released here. Which two numbers the ends of the color bar are set to decides how much of the colormap any part of the data actually reaches, and that choice changes a display at least as much as the choice of colors does.

The stretch and the data

Work through the four steps in order, then try the exercises.

1Where the ends of the color bar go

The histogram in the panel is the attribute's distribution across the survey, and the two red lines are the ends of the color bar. Everything between them is spread across the colormap. Everything outside is clipped: rendered in the end color and no longer distinguishable from anything else out there.

Two readouts carry the consequence. The clipped percentages say how much of the survey has been flattened at each end. The last one says how many of the colormap's 256 levels are reaching the middle half of the data, which is where most of it usually sits.

Set the range to everything, with the attribute on envelope. Almost nothing is clipped, and the middle half of the data receives 15 of the colormap's 256 levels, because the range is being held open by a small number of very high values along the channel axis. Clipping at the 2nd and 98th percentiles raises that to 21, and at the 5th and 95th to 24.

How much is gained depends entirely on how heavy the tails are, and the attributes differ a great deal. Mean curvature is the extreme case in this model: at the full range the middle half of the data gets 6 levels, and at a 5–95% clip it gets 50. The fault traces are what hold its range open, and they are a tiny fraction of the survey.

That trade is the whole of this module. Clipping discards distinctions above the limit; it buys distinctions below it. Neither direction is free and there is no setting that avoids the choice, including the default.

An automatic range set from the data's own minimum and maximum is not a neutral choice. It is the choice that gives the extreme samples the most influence over the display, and on most seismic attributes those extreme samples are the least representative part of the volume.

The channel margins are the place to watch. They are a gradual thinning, so they occupy a narrow band of envelope values a little above the background. Whether that band gets three colors or thirty decides whether the margin reads as a gradient or as a hard edge, and neither reading is in the data more than the other.

2A few bad samples

The slider in the panel puts a handful of bad samples into the attribute: forty traces out of thirty-two thousand, scaled by the amount selected. That is about one sample in eight hundred, which is a mild version of what a processing artefact, a bad trace or an acquisition footprint produces in real data.

With the range set to everything, the effect is immediate and out of all proportion. On envelope at a setting of 8, the middle half of the data falls from 15 levels of the colormap to 2. The channel, the faults and the pockmarks disappear together, and what is left is a nearly uniform field with forty bright dots in it. The attribute volume is unchanged; only two numbers have moved.

The fix is to stop setting the limits from the extreme values and set them from the distribution instead. A percentile clip puts the ends at chosen percentiles: the 2nd and 98th, for instance, which is to say that two per cent of the survey is deliberately clipped at each end. Forty bad samples out of thirty-two thousand are 0.12% and are comfortably inside that, so they are clipped and stop mattering.

The preset buttons run the common choices. The differences between them matter less than the difference between any of them and using the raw extremes.

SettingWhat it doesWhere it is appropriate
EverythingEnds at the data's own minimum and maximumWhen the extremes are the subject, and only then

5–95% or 10–90%Clips moreNoisy attributes, and displays meant to show the bulk rather than the extremes
±2 s.d.Symmetric about the mean, two standard deviations outAttributes that are roughly symmetric; poor on skewed ones

The standard deviation preset is worth trying on a skewed attribute to see it fail. Sweetness and envelope have a long tail toward high values, so their mean sits above the mode and two standard deviations reaches below zero at the bottom and not far enough at the top. Percentiles make no assumption about the shape of the distribution and do not have this problem.

3Stretching the middle

Clipping moves the ends. A stretch changes what happens between them: which parts of the range get many colors and which get few, without discarding anything.

Gamma raises the normalized value to a power before the colormap is looked up. Above one, the colors are spread over the high end of the range and the low end is compressed. Below one, the reverse. The histogram in the panel does not change, because the data has not changed; what changes is how many color levels each part of it receives.

A logarithmic stretch is the appropriate choice when an attribute spans orders of magnitude. Energy and envelope often do. A linear display of such an attribute spends most of its colors on the top decade and renders everything below it in a narrow band, which is not a defect of the colormap.

Two cautions come with any stretch, and both follow from module 03.

A stretch undoes perceptual uniformity. A colormap that was evenly spaced in the data is no longer evenly spaced once a gamma has been applied, and the strength of the stretch sets how badly. The color bar remains correctly labelled, and its labels are no longer evenly spaced along it, which is the visible symptom.

A stretch also moves any false boundary the colormap contains, in the same way that changing the range does. Running the module 03 test through a stretch rather than through the range gives the same answer: what moves belongs to the display.

Strong gamma settings are worth treating as a signal rather than a solution. An attribute that needs a gamma of 2.5 before its features appear is usually an attribute that would be better computed differently, better clipped, or displayed as a logarithm of itself with the color bar labelled that way and the fact stated.

4Gain on a section

On a vertical section the same choice appears under a different name. Amplitude is displayed at a clip level set as a multiple of the root mean square amplitude in the window, and turning that number down is what interpreters mean by turning the gain up.

The section in the panel is line 2000 across the channel. The clip level control sets the ends of the color bar at plus and minus that many times the section's own root mean square. The readout gives the percentage of samples being clipped.

At six times the root mean square almost nothing clips and the section looks flat and gray, because the strong channel reflection is holding the range open and the weaker events are compressed into a few colors near the middle. At one times the root mean square the section looks crisp and structural, the continuity of every reflector is easy to follow, and roughly a third of the samples have been flattened. Below that the display becomes close to a two-color rendering of the sign of the trace.

Which is right depends entirely on the question.

  • Following structure — a hard clip is genuinely better. Continuity is a question about where reflectors are, not how strong they are, and clipping removes an irrelevant variable.
  • Anything about amplitude — bright spots, dimming over a channel, tuning effects, amplitude against offset — a hard clip destroys the measurement. The channel sand in this model has a strong top reflection whose strength varies with the bed thickness, and at one times the root mean square that variation is entirely gone.

The trap is that these two displays are usually the same display. A section gained for picking gets used to assess an amplitude anomaly, and the anomaly has already been clipped away. Changing the gain and watching whether the anomaly changes with it is the check, and it is the same check as module 03: what moves when the display moves belongs to the display.

Why it matters

The value range is the part of a display most often left to a default and least often recorded. A figure in a report carries its colormap visibly, in the color bar, and carries its range as two numbers at the ends of that bar which nobody reads. Yet two displays of the same attribute at different ranges can support opposite conclusions about how far an anomaly extends.

Three specific failures are worth naming.

The automatic range. Software that sets the ends from the data's minimum and maximum has made the extreme samples the most influential part of the volume. On any attribute with a processing artefact or a bad trace in it, this compresses everything real into a fraction of the colormap.

The range that was right for the last volume. A range carried over from another survey, or from the same survey before reprocessing, will look plausible and be wrong, and there is nothing in the image to say so.

The comparison at two ranges. Comparing an attribute between two areas, two vintages or two processing products requires the same range on both. This is obvious when stated and easy to lose when the two displays were made in different sessions.

The practical answer is small: set the range from percentiles rather than extremes, keep it symmetric when the attribute has a meaningful zero, and write the two numbers in the figure caption along with the colormap name. Someone else can then rebuild the display and check it.

Exercises

  1. Buy colors with clipping. With envelope on the full range, note how many colors reach the middle half of the data. Set the 2–98% preset and note it again, then repeat the whole exercise with mean curvature.
    HintEnvelope goes from 15 levels to 21 for a cost of four per cent of the survey. Mean curvature goes from 6 to 21, and to 50 at a 5–95% clip, because its range is held open by the fault traces, which are a very small part of the survey and are already unmistakable. What is given up in both cases is the ability to distinguish values inside the clipped tails.
  2. Ruin a display with forty samples. Set the range to everything and raise the bad-sample slider to about 8.
    HintOne sample in eight hundred takes over the display completely: the limits stretch to accommodate them and everything else collapses into a few colors. Switching to a 2–98% clip restores the display almost exactly, because the bad samples are 0.12% of the survey and fall inside the clipped tails.
  3. Break the standard deviation preset. Put the attribute on sweetness and compare ±2 s.d. against 2–98%.
    HintSweetness has a long high tail, so its mean sits well above its mode and two standard deviations runs below zero at the bottom, wasting part of the colormap on values that do not exist, while stopping short at the top. The percentile clip makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution and handles the skew without being told about it.
  4. Watch a stretch undo the uniformity. Put the colormap on viridis, the stretch on gamma, and move the gamma to 2.5, watching the tick labels along the color bar.
    HintThe labels bunch up at one end. The colormap is still perceptually uniform in its own terms, but it is no longer evenly spaced in the data, which is what module 03 was measuring. A stretch trades the uniformity for emphasis on one part of the range, and that is a reasonable trade as long as it is deliberate.
  5. Gain a section two ways. On step 4, set the clip level to 6 and then to 1, and watch the top of the channel sand where the bed is thickest.
    HintAt 6 the reflection strength varies visibly along the channel as the bed thickens and thins through tuning. At 1 that variation has gone, because every strong sample is rendered in the same end color. The section at 1 is the better display for following reflectors and the worse one for saying anything at all about amplitude.
  6. Move a boundary with the stretch. Put the colormap on the rainbow with envelope displayed, find a band edge on the flank of the channel, then change the gamma.
    HintThe edge slides, exactly as it did when the range was changed in module 03. The test works on any change to the mapping between data and color: what stays put belongs to the data and what moves belongs to the display.
  7. A colleague's figure shows an amplitude anomaly covering a third of the survey. Which two settings would you ask for before believing it?
    HintThe range limits, and whether they came from percentiles or from the data's own extremes. An anomaly covering a third of a survey is often a range set so tightly that the bulk of the data saturates at one end, which turns ordinary background into an apparent feature. The stretch is the second thing to ask about, for the same reason. Neither is recoverable from the picture.
  • The value range decides how much of the colormap each part of the data reaches. Clipping discards distinctions outside the limits and buys distinctions inside them, and there is no setting that avoids the trade.
  • A range set from the data's own minimum and maximum gives the most extreme samples the most influence. A handful of bad samples can take over a display entirely.
  • Percentile clips make no assumption about the shape of the distribution. Standard-deviation clips do, and fail on skewed attributes.
  • A stretch changes the spacing within the range rather than the ends. It undoes perceptual uniformity, and the unevenly spaced tick labels on the color bar are the visible symptom.
  • Gain on a section is the same control. A hard clip helps with structure and destroys amplitude information, and one display is usually being asked to do both jobs.
  • Two numbers in the caption, alongside the colormap name, are enough for someone else to reproduce the display.

Method

The range sliders are expressed as percentages of the span the attribute actually covers in this survey, so that the whole range is reachable for every attribute without separate scales. The preset buttons compute percentiles from a sorted copy of the attribute, recomputed whenever the attribute or the bad-sample setting changes.

The bad samples in step 2 are placed at forty scattered map positions and scaled by the slider as a multiple of the attribute's own span. Forty of thirty-two thousand is 0.12% of the survey, which is why a 2% clip removes them without being aimed at them. They are added to the attribute after it is computed rather than to the model, so they behave like a display or processing artefact rather than like geology.

The stretches are applied by building a warped copy of the colormap rather than by transforming the data: level i of the warped table holds the color the original table had at position f(i), where f is the gamma power or the logarithm. The result is identical to transforming the data and looking the color up normally, and it keeps every other display in the site working with the same code. The logarithmic stretch uses a fixed base chosen so the bottom of the range stays finite.

The section clip level is expressed as a multiple of the root mean square amplitude of the section being displayed, which is recomputed for each line. This is the convention used for setting gain, and it makes the number comparable between sections with different absolute amplitudes. The percentage clipped is counted from the samples in that section.

Nothing in this module changes the model. The synthetic is the one described in module 00, at its default settings, and every figure here is the same data displayed differently.
